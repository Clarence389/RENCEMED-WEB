Regimen Class First-Line Preferred DAAs Genotype / Patient Profile Guideline Duration & Key Clinical Interactions
1. Preferred Pan-Genotypic Frameworks (Genotype-Independent)
First-Line Pan-Genotypic
Highly Preferred		 Sofosbuvir / Velpatasvir
(Epclusa) 		Genotypes 1–6
• Non-cirrhotic OR compensated cirrhotic patients. 		• Standard Duration: 12 weeks.
• Interaction Warning: Acid suppressants significantly lower Velpatasvir absorption. Antacids should be spaced by 4 hours; H2 blockers separated or limited; Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs) are strictly restricted or timed with food.
Glecaprevir / Pibrentasvir
(Mavyret) 		Genotypes 1–6
• Ideal choice if shorter therapeutic course is desired. 		• Standard Duration: 8 weeks (for treatment-naive patients with or without compensated cirrhosis).
• Interaction Warning: Ethinyl estradiol-containing contraceptives are contraindicated due to risk of ALT elevations. Statins (Atorvastatin, Simvastatin) are highly restricted due to toxic systemic accumulation.
2. Genotype-Specific / Alternative Single-Tablet Regimens
Genotype-Restricted Sofosbuvir / Ledipasvir
(Harvoni) 		Genotypes 1, 4, 5, 6
• Ineffective against Genotypes 2 and 3. 		• Duration: 8 to 12 weeks depending on baseline viral load and cirrhosis status.
• Interaction Warning: Shares major absorption interactions with acid-reducing agents (similar to Velpatasvir). Avoid combining with Amiodarone due to life-threatening bradycardia risks.
3. Universal Management Red Flags & Interventions
Universal DAA Interactions All core DAA treatments
• (Sofosbuvir, Glecaprevir, Velpatasvir backbones) 		Comorbid or specialized medications
  • Anticonvulsants: Carbamazepine, Phenytoin, and Phenobarbital induce P-gp/CYP, severely rendering DAAs ineffective. Contraindicated
  • Herbal Supplements: St. John’s Wort drastically minimizes systemic drug plasma levels. Contraindicated
  • Statins: DAA co-administration raises blood plasma levels of cholesterol therapeutics, escalating risks of severe rhabdomyolysis or muscle toxicity. Dose reduction required.
HBV Reactivation Risk Universal baseline precaution prior to initializing any DAA therapy HCV / HBV Co-infection Profile • Clinical Phenomenon: Curing the active Hepatitis C virus removes an immune suppression dynamic, which can trigger a rapid, catastrophic reactivation of latent Hepatitis B virus.
• Mandatory Action: Screen all patients for HBsAg and Anti-HBc prior to DAA onset. If latent HBV is confirmed, closely monitor HBV DNA levels or consider initiating concurrent anti-HBV prophylaxis.

Patient education Patient Education Guide: Hepatitis C
Education Pillar Core Clinical Concept Patient Actions & Educational Guidance
1. Understanding Diagnosis Curable viral liver infection Hepatitis C is a viral infection that causes inflammation of the liver tissue. It is historically a "silent" condition—meaning **you can live with the virus for decades without feeling sick**, while progressive liver scarring (fibrosis) slowly damages your organs.
2. Purpose of Treatment Complete Viral Cure Unlike standard management for other types of hepatitis, **Hepatitis C is fully curable.** Modern treatments use daily Direct-Acting Antiviral (DAA) tablets.
  • Treatment lasts only **8 to 12 weeks** for most individuals.
  • It completely eradicates the virus from your body, halting further liver damage.
  • Curing the virus drops your long-term risks of liver failure and liver cancer.
3. Absolute Adherence Flawless daily execution You must take your medication **every single day, exactly as prescribed, without skipping doses.**
Failure Risk Missing pills gives the virus an opportunity to mutate, causing the medication to stop working permanently (drug resistance). If you accidentally miss a dose, check your package instructions or call your clinic immediately.
4. Lifestyle Measures Liver protection & prevention
  • **Zero Alcohol:** Alcohol accelerates liver damage aggressively when paired with Hepatitis C. Stop drinking during treatment, and ideally indefinitely if scarring is advanced.
  • **Check Drug Interactions:** Common items like antacids, proton pump inhibitors, and herbal remedies (like St. John’s Wort) can completely neutralize your virus medication. Consult your pharmacist before starting *any* new substance.
  • **Prevent Transmission & Re-infection:** The virus is transmitted through blood-to-blood contact. Do not share razors, toothbrushes, or injection equipment. Note that **curing the virus does not make you immune;** you can catch Hepatitis C again if re-exposed.
5. Monitoring Requirements SVR12 verification & scar tracking
  • **The "Cure Test" (SVR12):** You must return for a blood draw **12 weeks after finishing your entire pill course.** If no virus is found in your blood at this point, you are officially cured.
  • **Long-Term Monitoring:** If you have advanced liver scarring or cirrhosis (F3-F4), **you must continue to get a liver ultrasound every 6 months indefinitely**, even after you are cured, to monitor for liver nodules.
6. Seek Urgent Care Decompensation Red Flags Go to the emergency department or notify your care team immediately if you develop signs of serious liver distress or drug toxicity:
  • **Jaundice:** Sudden yellowing of your eyes or your skin.
  • **Elimination shifts:** Urine that appears deep brown/tea-colored or stool that becomes pale gray/clay-colored.
  • **Abdominal shifts:** Rapid, painful swelling or fluid buildup in your abdomen (ascites).
  • **Severe Systemic Issues:** Persistent, intractable vomiting or severe dizziness.
Multidisciplinary rolesDoctors assess and diagnose; pharmacists optimize medicines, identify interactions and support adherence; nurses monitor, educate and coordinate care; patients participate in decisions, take medicines as agreed and report problems promptly.
Individualization Hepatitis C: Individualization of Therapy
Patient Factor Clinical Scenario Individualized Management Strategy & Selection Rationale
Age Considerations Pediatric (≥3 years) vs. Older adults • Pediatric Patients: DAA regimens (Sofosbuvir/Velpatasvir or Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir) are approved down to 3 years of age, utilizing weight-based granules or lower-dose tablets.
• Older Adults: Highly eligible for simplified 8- or 12-week regimens. Polypharmacy risks are elevated; comprehensive review of standard age-related medications (e.g., cardiovascular medications, sedatives) is mandatory before starting.
Pregnancy & Lactation Active maternal infection during childbearing cycle • Guideline Standard: Direct-Acting Antiviral (DAA) therapy is generally not recommended during pregnancy due to limited human safety data.
• Strategy: Defer treatment until postpartum. Screen infants born to HCV-positive mothers at age 18 months using an antibody test, or at 2 months using an HCV RNA test if early clearance verification is desired.
Renal & Hepatic Function Severe kidney disease (dialysis) or decompensated cirrhosis • Severe Renal Impairment (eGFR <30 or ESRD): Both standard first-line options (Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir and Sofosbuvir/Velpatasvir) are now fully approved for use in patients on dialysis without any dosage changes.
• Decompensated Cirrhosis (Child-Pugh B or C): Absolute Contraindication: Protease inhibitors (Glecaprevir, Voxilaprevir) are strictly contraindicated due to risk of severe, hepatotoxic drug accumulation. Treat only with Sofosbuvir/Velpatasvir for 24 weeks, or 12 weeks combined with Ribavirin.
Comorbidities & Concurrent Medications HIV or HBV co-infection; Acid-suppression user • HIV Co-infection: Curable with standard DAAs, but requires extensive drug-drug interaction screening against antiretroviral therapies (ART). Avoid combining Tenofovir Disoproxil Fumarate (TDF) with Sofosbuvir-led regimens to protect kidney health.
• HBV Co-infection: Curing HCV can trigger an aggressive reactivation of latent HBV. You must check for HBsAg prior to treatment.
• Acid-Suppressants: If a patient relies heavily on PPIs for severe GERD, choose Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir (which does not interact with gastric pH) over Sofosbuvir/Velpatasvir.
Allergies & Intolerances History of severe hypersensitivity reactions • True allergies to modern DAAs are exceedingly rare. If a documented allergic reaction to a specific drug class occurs (e.g., an NS5A inhibitor), switch to an alternative combination regimen entirely (e.g., swapping a Sofosbuvir/Velpatasvir plan for a Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir course).
Socioeconomic Barriers Uninsured status, unstable housing, or active substance use • Harm Reduction: Active injection drug use is NOT a reason to withhold treatment. Guidelines strongly advocate for immediate treatment to eliminate community transmission.
• Cost & Supply Barriers: Utilize simplified 8-week courses (Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir) to lower the burden of clinical appointments. Coordinate with patient assistance programs or medication coordinators early to secure the full 8- or 12-week drug supply prior to the patient swallowing their first dose.
Patient Preferences Pill burden comfort and daily lifestyle priorities • Pill Volume: Sofosbuvir/Velpatasvir is an attractive 1-pill, once-daily choice for 12 weeks. Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir requires 3 small pills taken together once daily, but offers a shorter 8-week duration.
• Food Requirements: Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir must be taken with food for proper absorption, whereas Sofosbuvir/Velpatasvir can be taken with or without food, adapting better to variable schedules.

Patient Checklist

  • Know the name and purpose of each medicine.
  • Know how and when to take or use treatment.
  • Ask what adverse effects require medical attention.
  • Keep appointments and monitoring tests.
  • Tell the healthcare team about OTC medicines, supplements and allergies.
  • Do not stop long-term treatment abruptly without professional advice unless an emergency clinician instructs otherwise.
