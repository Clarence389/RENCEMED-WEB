Disease-Specific Clinical Table

Clinical domainReference notes
Diagnosis / assessmentClinical assessment; Validated Screening Tools: Standardized psychometric scales assist in quantifying symptom severity and tracking treatment outcomes over time.GAD-7 (Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7): A brief, 7-item scale highly effective for screening and tracking severity.HAM-A (Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale): A clinician-administered scale that thoroughly breaks down psychic versus somatic symptoms.PDSS (Panic Disorder Severity Scale): Used specifically to quantify the frequency and impairment of panic attacks.
Key monitoringSymptoms; Psychic Symptoms: Assess the nature of the cognitive burden. Evaluate for pervasive, uncontrollable worry (indicative of Generalized Anxiety Disorder), intrusive obsessions (OCD), or an acute dread of negative evaluation (Social Anxiety Disorder). Somatic Symptoms: Quantify physical hyperarousal. Document autonomic signs such as tachycardia, diaphoresis, muscle tension, tremors, and gastrointestinal distress, function, sleep, substance use and treatment response
Management principlesPsychological therapies and selected medicines when indicated; First-Line Evidence-Based Psychological TherapiesPsychotherapy addresses the underlying cognitive distortions and behavioral avoidance patterns driving the anxiety.Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT):Core Mechanism: Focuses on identifying, challenging, and restructuring maladaptive, catastrophic thought patterns (cognitive restructuring) and altering behavioral responses.Exposure Therapy / Response Prevention: The gold standard for phobias, panic disorder, and OCD. Patients are systematically exposed to feared stimuli or somatic sensations (like elevated heart rate) in a controlled environment to promote habituation and extinction of the fear response.
Medication Class & Examples Primary Indications Clinical Rationale & Mechanism Key Side Effects & Clinical Risks
SSRIs
(e.g., Escitalopram, Sertraline) 		First-line for GAD, Panic Disorder, SAD, and OCD. Downregulates presynaptic serotonin receptors over time, stabilizing emotional processing. Takes 4–6 weeks for therapeutic effect. Temporary worsening of anxiety upon initiation, sexual dysfunction, gastrointestinal upset, weight changes.
SNRIs
(e.g., Venlafaxine, Duloxetine) 		First-line for GAD, Panic Disorder, and SAD. Inhibits both serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake, providing broader neurotransmitter modulation. Dose-dependent increases in blood pressure, sweating, and severe withdrawal symptoms if discontinued abruptly.
Beta-Blockers
(e.g., Propranolol) 		Performance anxiety, Social Anxiety (Somatic symptoms). Purely targets somatic anxiety. Blocks peripheral beta-adrenergic receptors to blunt tachycardia, tremors, and sweating without affecting cognitive worry. Hypotension, bradycardia, fatigue. Contraindicated in patients with severe asthma.
Gabapentinoids
(e.g., Pregabalin) 		GAD (Commonly utilized in European/international guidelines). Binds to voltage-gated calcium channels in the CNS, reducing the release of excitatory neurotransmitters like glutamate. Somnolence, dizziness, weight gain, and potential for misuse or physical dependence.
Benzodiazepines
(e.g., Alprazolam, Clonazepam) 		Short-term crisis management, acute panic, bridging SSRI titration. High risk of total therapeutic failure if used long-term. Positive allosteric modulator of GABA-A receptors, causing immediate central nervous system depression. Severe risks: Rapid development of tolerance, profound physical and psychological dependence, cognitive impairment, and a dangerous, potentially life-threatening withdrawal syndrome.
Follow-up / outcomesResponse, adverse effects, dependence risk for sedatives Common / Expected Effects: Daytime somnolence, dizziness, slowed reaction times, ataxia (impaired coordination), anterograde amnesia, and mild cognitive slowing.Severe Physical Risks:Respiratory Depression: Profound depression of the brainstem respiratory drive, which is exponentially magnified if combined with other CNS depressants like alcohol or opioids.Paradoxical Reactions: Hostility, severe aggression, and uninhibited emotional outbursts (occurring more frequently in pediatric and geriatric populations).Geriatric Vulnerabilities: Significant increases in the incidence of falls, motor vehicle accidents, hip fractures, and the exacerbation of pre-existing neurocognitive disorders (dementia).3. Tolerance, Dependence, and Withdrawal RisksPhysiological dependence can develop within less than 14 days of continuous, high-dose sedative use. The process occurs across three specific clinical phases:Sedative Dependency Progression Framework [ Receptor Downregulation ] ──> [ Tolerance Development ] ──> [ Severe Physical Withdrawal ] (GABA-A Desensitization) (Higher Doses Required)
Patient educationExplain the diagnosis, purpose of treatment, adherence, lifestyle measures, monitoring requirements and when to seek urgent care. Use teach-back to confirm understanding.
Multidisciplinary rolesDoctors assess and diagnose; pharmacists optimize medicines, identify interactions and support adherence; nurses monitor, educate and coordinate care; patients participate in decisions, take medicines as agreed and report problems promptly.
IndividualizationConsider age, pregnancy, comorbidities, renal/hepatic function, allergies, concurrent medicines, socioeconomic barriers and patient preferences.

Patient Checklist

  • Know the name and purpose of each medicine.
  • Know how and when to take or use treatment.
  • Ask what adverse effects require medical attention.
  • Keep appointments and monitoring tests.
  • Tell the healthcare team about OTC medicines, supplements and allergies.
  • Do not stop long-term treatment abruptly without professional advice unless an emergency clinician instructs otherwise.

🚨 When urgent medical care may be needed

Severe distress with inability to maintain safety or acute medical symptoms
