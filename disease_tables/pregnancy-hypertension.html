Clinical framework

What clinicians assess

This page organizes the major clinical decision points for Hypertensive Disorders of Pregnancy. It is designed for patient education and professional revision, not as a substitute for a current guideline, local protocol or individualized clinical assessment.

Diagnosis / assessment
Diagnostic Component Diagnostic Criteria & Parameters Clinical Utility & Evaluation Focus
Blood Pressure (BP) Measurement
  • Hypertension Threshold: SBP ≥ 140 mmHg or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg on two occasions at least 4 hours apart after 20 weeks gestation.
  • Severe Range BP: SBP ≥ 160 mmHg or DBP ≥ 110 mmHg (confirmed within 15 minutes to initiate urgent therapy).
  • Establishes baseline severity and differentiates gestational hypertension from chronic hypertension (onset before 20 weeks).
  • Proper cuff size and seated patient positioning are required for accurate readings.
Urine Protein & Renal Assessment
  • Proteinuria Criteria: Spot urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (UPCR) ≥ 0.3 mg/mg, 24-hour urine protein ≥ 300 mg, or urine dipstick ≥ 2+ (if quantitative test unavailable).
  • Renal Dysfunction: Serum creatinine > 1.1 mg/dL or doubling of serum creatinine in the absence of other renal disease.
  • Differentiates gestational hypertension (no proteinuria) from preeclampsia.
  • Quantifies renal end-organ involvement in preeclampsia.
Maternal Systemic & Laboratory Workup
  • Thrombocytopenia: Platelet count < 100,000/μL.
  • Hepatic Dysfunction: Serum transaminases (AST/ALT) ≥ 2× upper limit of normal; severe persistent right upper quadrant / epigastric pain.
  • Neurological / Visual Symptoms: New-onset persistent headache, scotomata, altered mental status, or hyperreflexia/clonus.
  • Pulmonary Edema: Acute dyspnea, hypoxia, or rales on auscultation.
  • Identifies severe features of preeclampsia and assesses for HELLP syndrome (Hemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, Low Platelets).
  • Aids in rapid decision-making regarding ICU transfer and delivery timing.
Fetal Wellbeing Assessment
  • Ultrasound Evaluation: Fetal growth restriction (EFW < 10th percentile), oligohydramnios (AFI < 5 cm or DVP < 2 cm).
  • Doppler Studies: Umbilical artery Doppler velocimetry (absent or reversed end-diastolic flow indicates high fetal risk).
  • Antepartum Surveillance: Non-stress test (NST) and Biophysical Profile (BPP).
  • Detects placental insufficiency, fetal compromise, and growth restriction secondary to maternal vasospasm.
  • Guides multidisciplinary decisions regarding fetal viability, inpatient monitoring, or urgent delivery.
Key monitoring
Monitoring Domain Clinical Indicators & Targets Frequency & Escalation Thresholds
Blood Pressure (BP) Surveillance
  • Target Range: Maintain SBP 135–150 mmHg and DBP 85–95 mmHg (prevent maternal cerebral hemorrhage while preserving uteroplacental perfusion).
  • Severe Threshold: Acute SBP ≥ 160 mmHg or DBP ≥ 110 mmHg.
  • Outpatient / Gestational HTN: 1–2 times weekly.
  • Inpatient / Preeclampsia: Every 15–30 minutes during acute severe crises; every 4 hours once stabilized.
  • Escalation: Severe BP sustained for ≥ 15 minutes requires immediate IV antihypertensive treatment.
Symptom & Neurological Check
  • Warning Signs: Persistent frontal headache, visual changes (blurred vision, scotomata), right upper quadrant (RUQ) / epigastric pain, or acute dyspnea.
  • Reflexes: Patellar deep tendon reflexes and hyperreflexia/clonus.
  • Evaluate symptom check and patellar reflexes at every BP assessment or shift change.
  • Escalation: Rapidly worsening neurological symptoms or brisk clonus signals eclampsia risk—initiate MgSO4 prophylaxis and order urgent labs.
Urine Protein & Organ Function Labs
  • Platelet Count: Target > 100,000/μL.
  • Liver & Kidney Markers: Stable AST, ALT, LDH, Serum Creatinine, and Uric Acid.
  • Urine Protein: Dipstick protein check or spot UPCR (used for baseline diagnosis; repeated quantitative urine testing is generally not required if diagnosis is established).
  • Outpatient Preeclampsia: Weekly or bi-weekly complete blood count (CBC) and liver/renal profile.
  • Inpatient / Severe Preeclampsia: Daily laboratory testing or every 12 hours if HELLP syndrome is suspected.
  • Escalation: Platelets < 100,000/μL or doubling of liver enzymes warrants delivery considerations.
Maternal-Fetal Status & Ultrasound
  • Fetal Movement: Daily maternal fetal movement (kick counts).
  • Biophysical Profile / NST: Reactive non-stress test and amniotic fluid volume (AFI / DVP).
  • Doppler Assessment: Umbilical artery Doppler velocimetry.
  • NST / AFI: 1–2 times weekly in stable preeclampsia; daily or continuous in severe inpatient cases.
  • Ultrasound Growth / Doppler: Every 2–4 weeks.
  • Escalation: Non-reactive NST, oligohydramnios, or absent/reversed end-diastolic flow requires emergency delivery evaluation.
Management principles
Therapeutic Pillar Pregnancy-Safe Regimens & Dosing Clinical Strategy & Practice Directives
Acute Severe Blood Pressure Control
  • IV Labetalol: 20 mg IV bolus over 2 min; repeat 40–80 mg q10–20 min (max 300 mg total).
  • IV Hydralazine: 5–10 mg IV over 2 min; repeat 5–10 mg q20–30 min (max 30 mg total).
  • Oral Nifedipine (Immediate-Release): 10–20 mg PO; repeat in 20–30 min if needed.
  • Indicated for acute severe hypertension (SBP ≥ 160 mmHg or DBP ≥ 110 mmHg sustained for 15+ min).
  • Target: Gradual lowering to SBP 140–150 mmHg and DBP 90–100 mmHg to avoid acute fetal distress from uteroplacental hypoperfusion.
Maintenance Oral Antihypertensive Therapy
  • Labetalol: 100–400 mg PO BID or TID (max 2,400 mg/day).
  • Nifedipine Extended-Release (ER): 30–60 mg PO daily (max 120 mg/day).
  • Methyldopa: 250 mg PO BID/TID (max 3,000 mg/day).
  • Contraindicated: ACE inhibitors, ARBs, Direct Renin Inhibitors, and Mineralocorticoid Receptor Antagonists (fetotoxicity/renal dysgenesis).
  • Initiate for persistent non-severe hypertension (SBP ≥ 140–150 mmHg or DBP ≥ 90–100 mmHg) to reduce maternal stroke risk.
  • Titrate doses gradually to maintain BP within target safe range (SBP 135–150 mmHg / DBP 85–95 mmHg).
Eclampsia Prophylaxis & Neuroprotection
  • Magnesium Sulfate (MgSO4) Loading Dose: 4–6 g IV in 100 mL IV fluid over 15–20 minutes.
  • MgSO4 Maintenance Dose: 1–2 g/hour continuous IV infusion for 24 hours post-delivery or post-seizure.
  • Toxicity Antidote: Calcium gluconate 1 g IV over 3–5 min for respiratory depression/loss of patellar reflexes.
  • First-line agent for seizure prevention in preeclampsia with severe features or eclampsia treatment.
  • Provides fetal neuroprotection against cerebral palsy when delivered prior to 32 weeks gestation.
Obstetric Strategy & Delivery Timing
  • Gestational HTN / Preeclampsia without Severe Features: Delivery recommended at 37 0/7 weeks gestation.
  • Preeclampsia with Severe Features: Delivery recommended at 34 0/7 weeks gestation (or immediately if maternal/fetal instability occurs).
  • Antenatal Corticosteroids: Betamethasone 12 mg IM q24h × 2 doses if delivery expected before 34 weeks for fetal lung maturity.
  • Delivery is the definitive cure for preeclampsia; balance maternal risks against fetal prematurity.
  • Vaginal delivery is preferred unless obstetric indications require a Cesarean section.
Follow-up / outcomes
Follow-up Phase Key Monitoring & Laboratory Indicators Target Outcomes & Escalation Directives
Immediate Postpartum Surveillance (Days 1–7)
  • Blood Pressure Checks: Monitor BP q4h for at least 72 hours post-delivery and again 7–10 days postpartum.
  • Laboratory Normalization: Repeat CBC (platelet recovery) and liver/renal function panels prior to discharge.
  • Be alert for postpartum preeclampsia or eclampsia (which can present up to 6 weeks delivery).
  • Reinitiate or titrate oral antihypertensives if BP remains ≥ 150/100 mmHg.
Subacute Postpartum Review (Weeks 2–6)
  • BP Tapering & Weaning: Assess suitability for tapering antihypertensive therapy as maternal vascular resistance normalizes.
  • Proteinuria & Renal Resolution: Check repeat urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (UPCR) at 6 weeks postpartum.
  • Target: Complete resolution of gestational hypertension or preeclampsia features by 12 weeks postpartum.
  • Persistent hypertension past 12 weeks indicates underlying chronic hypertension requiring long-term primary care referral.
Neonatal & Fetal Well-being Outcome
  • Neonatal Assessment: Evaluate for complications related to prematurity, low birth weight, or intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR).
  • Pediatric Follow-up: Growth charting and developmental milestone surveillance.
  • Ensure smooth transition of care to pediatric specialists for premature or small-for-gestational-age infants.
Long-Term Maternal Cardiovascular Health
  • Cardiovascular Risk Screening: Annual BP check, lipid profile, fast glucose/HbA1c, and renal function screening.
  • Recurrence Planning: Pre-conception counseling for future pregnancies.
  • Educate patient that preeclampsia doubles the long-term risk for premature ischemic heart disease, stroke, and chronic hypertension.
  • Recommend daily low-dose aspirin (81–162 mg) starting at 12–16 weeks gestation in subsequent pregnancies.
Patient education
Education Domain Core Patient Counseling Messages Verification & Action Plan
Diagnosis & Purpose of Treatment
  • Diagnosis Explanation: Reassure and clarify that high blood pressure during pregnancy (gestational hypertension or preeclampsia) affects blood flow to the placenta and organs.
  • Treatment Purpose: Explain that blood pressure medication protects the mother from stroke and organ strain while keeping the baby safe until delivery.
  • Emphasize that delivery is the ultimate cure, but medications keep both mother and baby safe in the meantime.
  • Provide plain-language written leaflets detailing high blood pressure in pregnancy.
Medication Adherence & Safety
  • Daily Compliance: Take prescribed pregnancy-safe antihypertensives (e.g., Labetalol, Nifedipine) strictly as scheduled without skipping doses.
  • Avoiding Unsafe Drugs: Never take over-the-counter NSAIDs (like ibuprofen) or unprescribed blood pressure pills.
  • Establish a daily medication schedule aligned with daily routines.
  • Advise taking BP readings at home before medication if instructed by the clinical team.
Lifestyle Measures & Home Monitoring
  • Rest & Positioning: Rest frequently, preferably lying on the left side to improve blood flow to the placenta.
  • Fetal Movement Tracking: Count fetal movements ("kick counts") daily at the same time.
  • BP Monitoring: Log home blood pressure readings accurately using a validated upper-arm cuff.
  • Demonstrate proper BP cuff positioning and resting 5 minutes before taking a reading.
  • Instruct patient to contact clinic if fetal kicks are fewer than 10 in 2 hours.
Urgent Red Flags & Teach-Back Confirmation
  • Seek Emergency Care Immediately For:
    • Severe, persistent headache that doesn't go away with acetaminophen.
    • Visual changes (flashing lights, spots, blurred vision).
    • Upper stomach / right upper quadrant pain under the ribs.
    • Sudden swelling of face or hands, or severe shortness of breath.
  • Teach-Back Confirmation: Ask the patient to state in their own words:
    • "What warning signs mean you should go straight to the labor ward or emergency room?"
    • "How often will you take your blood pressure medication?"
    • "What should you do if you notice your baby is moving less than usual?"
Multidisciplinary roles
Team Member Primary Responsibilities Collaborative Focus & Care Delivery
Obstetricians & Physicians
  • Assessment & Diagnosis: Evaluate maternal BP, organ function, and diagnostic criteria for gestational HTN vs. preeclampsia.
  • Clinical Decisions: Determine timing of delivery, manage acute severe hypertensive crises, and oversee eclampsia protocols.
  • Coordinate care with maternal-fetal medicine (MFM) specialists, neonatologists, and anesthesiologists for high-risk delivery planning.
Clinical Pharmacists
  • Medication Optimization: Recommend pregnancy-safe antihypertensive agents, verify dosing accuracy, and screen for teratogenic drugs (e.g., ACEi/ARBs)[cite: 2].
  • Safety & Adherence: Identify drug interactions, manage magnesium sulfate protocols, and support patient adherence[cite: 2].
  • Guide acute antihypertensive titration protocols and advise on postpartum medication adjustments and breastfeeding safety[cite: 2].
Nurses & Midwives
  • Surveillance & Monitoring: Perform frequent BP measurements, assess deep tendon reflexes/clonus, and monitor magnesium toxicity[cite: 2].
  • Education & Care Coordination: Educate patients on warning signs, track intake/output, and monitor continuous fetal heart rates[cite: 2].
  • Serve as frontline clinical observers, triggering immediate medical response for severe BP spikes or acute symptoms[cite: 2].
Patient & Support Network
  • Shared Decision-Making: Participate actively in delivery timing and care plan discussions[cite: 2].
  • Adherence & Reporting: Take prescribed medications consistently and report red flag symptoms (headache, vision changes, stomach pain) promptly[cite: 2].
  • Perform home BP monitoring and fetal kick counts as directed to ensure maternal-fetal safety between appointments[cite: 2].
Individualization
Individualization Variable Clinical Assessment & Risk Modifiers Tailored Management Adjustments
Maternal Age & Gestational Stage
  • Advanced Maternal Age (≥ 35 yrs): Higher baseline baseline vascular disease risk and underlying chronic hypertension.
  • Gestational Age Context: Early-onset preeclampsia (< 34 weeks) carries significantly higher maternal-fetal morbidity than late-onset (≥ 37 weeks).
  • In early-onset preeclampsia, attempt expectant management with antenatal corticosteroids and MFM consultation if maternal-fetal conditions permit.
Comorbidities, Renal & Hepatic Function
  • Pre-existing Conditions: Pre-gestational diabetes, chronic kidney disease, or autoimmune disease (e.g., Lupus)[cite: 2].
  • Renal / Hepatic Impairment: Serum creatinine > 1.1 mg/dL or transaminases ≥ 2× upper limit of normal[cite: 2].
  • Adjust magnesium sulfate dosing in renal impairment (reduce maintenance or monitor serum magnesium levels to prevent toxicity)[cite: 2].
  • Avoid labetalol in patients with severe asthma or second/third-degree heart block[cite: 2].
Allergies & Concurrent Medications
  • Hypersensitivity Profile: Asthma or reactive airway disease contraindicates non-selective beta-blockers (Labetalol)[cite: 2].
  • Concurrent Therapies: Aspirin prophylaxis, insulin, or immunosuppressants[cite: 2].
  • Switch to Nifedipine ER or Methyldopa if Labetalol is contraindicated[cite: 2].
  • Verify all concurrent medications for fetal toxicity and drug-drug interactions[cite: 2].
Socioeconomic Barriers & Patient Preferences
  • Access Barriers: Inability to afford home blood pressure monitors, transportation limits for frequent outpatient monitoring, or medication costs[cite: 2].
  • Patient Goals & Cultural Values: Birth plan preferences and health literacy levels[cite: 2].
  • Provide subsidized home BP monitors, utilize community health nursing, and select once-daily oral regimens (e.g., Nifedipine ER) to enhance adherence[cite: 2].
  • Engage in shared decision-making regarding induction of labor vs. conservative care[cite: 2].

Patient Checklist

  • Know the name and purpose of each medicine.
  • Know how and when to take or use treatment.
  • Ask what adverse effects require medical attention.
  • Keep appointments and monitoring tests.
  • Tell the healthcare team about OTC medicines, supplements and allergies.
  • Do not stop long-term treatment abruptly without professional advice unless an emergency clinician instructs otherwise.

🚨 When urgent medical care may be needed

Severe headache, visual symptoms, RUQ pain, seizures, severe BP elevation

Emergency symptoms should be assessed urgently rather than managed from this webpage.
