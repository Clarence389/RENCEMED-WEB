What clinicians assess

This page organizes the major clinical decision points for Depressive Disorder. It is designed for patient education and professional revision, not as a substitute for a current guideline, local protocol or individualized clinical assessment.

Diagnosis / assessment
Assessment Domain Clinical Indicators & Diagnostic Criteria Validated Tools & Protocols
Symptom Burden & Severity
  • **Core Symptoms:* Persistent depressed mood or anhedonia (loss of interest/pleasure) lasting ≥2 weeks.
  • **Secondary Symptoms:* Changes in sleep/appetite, psychomotor agitation/retardation, fatigue, feelings of worthlessness, or impaired concentration.
  • **Severity Grading:* Categorized as Mild, Moderate, or Severe based on total symptom count and intensity.
  • Utilize standardized rating scales to quantify baseline severity: *PHQ-9* (Patient Health Questionnaire) for self-reporting.
  • In clinical or research settings, administer clinician-rated options like the **HAM-D** (Hamilton Depression Rating Scale) or **MADRS**.
Functional Status
  • Evaluate the impact of depressive symptoms on crucial domains of daily living.
  • Assess occupational capacity, educational consistency, interpersonal relationship maintenance, and basic self-care navigation (ADLs).
  • Incorporate the *WSAS* (Work and Social Adjustment Scale) or the **WHODAS 2.0** (World Health Organization Disability Assessment Schedule) to objectively monitor functional decline.
  • Establish concrete functional baselines to gauge recovery goals.
Suicide Risk Evaluation
  • *The Critical Safety Domain:* Proactively explore suicidal ideation, intent, plans, access to lethal means, and historical self-harm behaviors.
  • Identify compounding risk factors: severe agitation, concurrent substance abuse, acute hopelessness, or recent major life losses.
  • *Mandatory Protocol:* Administer the **C-SSRS** (Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale) or **SAFE-T** framework at initial presentation and every subsequent encounter.
  • Differentiate between active intent and passive ideation to safely guide triage levels (outpatient vs. inpatient safety protocols).
Key monitoring
Monitoring Domain Clinical Indicators & Surveillance Targets Evaluation Protocol & Frequency
Symptoms & Functional Recovery
  • **Treatment Response:** Seek a ≥50% reduction in baseline symptom severity.
  • **Remission Goal:** Achieve complete or near-complete resolution of depressive features (e.g., PHQ-9 < 5).
  • **Functional Trajectory:** Monitor return to occupational, educational, and interpersonal baselines.
  • Readminister the **PHQ-9** or **HAM-D** at every scheduled visit to objectively measure progress.
  • Evaluate intervals: every 1–2 weeks during initial treatment or titration phase; then every 1–3 months once stable.
Medication Adherence
  • Confirm consistent, daily antidepressant intake as prescribed.
  • Identify common barriers to adherence, such as delayed therapeutic onset (typically 4–6 weeks), stigma, forgetfulness, or cost.
  • Routinely use non-judgmental, open-ended screening questions (e.g., *"Many people find it hard to take pills every single day. How many doses have you missed in the last week?"*).
  • Perform tablet counts or pharmacy refill checks if adherence is in doubt.
Adverse Effects
  • **Early Effects:** Screen for temporary symptoms like nausea, mild headaches, GI upset, or transient anxiety.
  • **Long-Term Effects:** Track sexual dysfunction, weight gain, emotional blunting, sleep disturbances, or sedation.
  • **Lab Monitoring:** Check for drug-specific reactions (e.g., hyponatremia with SSRIs in older adults, or QTc prolongation).
  • Incorporate targeted questionnaires like the **FIBSER** (Frequency, Intensity, and Burden of Side Effects Rating) scale.
  • Routinely review dynamic side-effect trade-offs before considering an alternative medication class.
Safety & Suicide Risk
  • **The Critical Paradox:** Monitor closely for a paradoxical rise in energy, agitation, or suicidal ideation during the first 1–2 weeks of drug initiation (as motor retardation improves before mood lifts).
  • Evaluate for signs of **Serotonin Syndrome** or emergence of hypomanic/manic switches.
  • **Mandatory Protocol:** Re-evaluate suicide safety profiles using the **C-SSRS** at every clinical touchpoint.
  • Establish and verify a concrete **Safety Plan** with the patient and involve family/support figures whenever risk indices change.
Management principles
Severity Stratification Psychological Interventions Pharmacotherapy Interventions
Mild Severity
  • **First-Line Preference:** Offer evidence-based psychotherapy as an equivalent or preferred option over medication.
  • Incorporate low-intensity interventions: digital Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), guided self-help, or structured exercise frameworks.
  • Provide short-term face-to-face CBT or Interpersonal Psychotherapy (IPT) based on choice.
  • Routine antidepressant initiation is **not generally recommended** as a first-line approach unless dictated by specific clinical modifiers.
  • Consider prescribing only if the patient has a history of severe depressive episodes, exhibits an inadequate response to initial therapy, or explicitly requests it.
Moderate Severity
  • **Active Treatment:** Offer high-intensity formal psychological therapy (CBT, IPT, or Behavioral Activation).
  • Can be applied as a standalone treatment if the patient holds a strong preference against medication and has no immediate risk markers.
  • **Combination Choice:** Offer an antidepressant as a core first-line option, ideally combined with psychotherapy to achieve better long-term functional recovery.
  • Initiate first-line agents with favorable safety profiles: SSRIs (e.g., escitalopram, sertraline) or SNRIs (e.g., duloxetine).
Severe Severity
(Without/With Psychosis)
  • **Adjuvant Treatment:** Psychotherapy should be initiated **only in combination** with pharmacotherapy, never as a standalone approach.
  • Focus sessions on safety planning and supportive engagement during the acute crisis phase, moving to intensive CBT once cognitive clarity stabilizes.
  • **Mandatory Primary Treatment:** Immediate pharmacotherapy is required. Utilize optimized therapeutic doses of SSRIs, SNRIs, or dual-action agents (mirtazapine).
  • **Severe/Psychotic Modifiers:** If psychotic features are present, combine the antidepressant with a second-generation antipsychotic (e.g., aripiprazole, olanzapine).
  • Evaluate for urgent neurostimulation techniques (ECT) if the patient exhibits severe treatment resistance or life-threatening food refusal.
Patient Preference Integration
  • **Shared Decision-Making:** Actively incorporate the patient's past treatment experiences, anticipated side-effect tolerances (such as avoiding weight gain or sexual dysfunction), and financial constraints.
  • **Adherence Engineering:** If a patient expresses strong aversion to daily pill burdens, prioritize standalone psychotherapy for mild/moderate states. If medication is strictly required, establish a highly transparent side-effect monitoring protocol to preserve trust.
Follow-up / outcomes
Follow-up Domain Target Outcomes & Status Indicators Clinical Monitoring Protocol
Mood & Suicidality Tracker
  • **Mood Stabilization:** Gradual reduction in core depressive symptoms toward full remission (PHQ-9 < 5).
  • **Suicidality Resolution:** Complete containment of suicidal intent or planning.
  • **The Critical Paradox Interval:** Mandate evaluation within 1–2 weeks of initiating or augmenting treatment. Monitor for emerging self-harm intent as cognitive retardation resolves before emotional improvement lifts.
  • Readminister the **PHQ-9** and **C-SSRS** at every clinical intersection.
Behavioral Activation
  • **Functional Recovery:** Re-engagement in daily self-care routines, hobbies, social structures, and occupational commitments.
  • Reversal of psychomotor withdrawal features.
  • Track real-world activation markers using self-reported diary tracking or standardized scales like the **Behavioral Activation for Depression Scale (BADS)**.
  • Differentiate between chemical energy restoration (activation) and a potential conversion to mixed manic features.
Adverse Effects Surveillance
  • **Tolerability Benchmarks:** Management or attenuation of early somatic anomalies (nausea, agitation) and late adjustments (sexual dysfunction, weight gain).
  • Early detection of metabolic shifts.
  • Routinely review side effect severity profiles using targeted tools like the **FIBSER** questionnaire.
  • Obtain blood serum electrolyte measurements to screen for **antidepressant-induced hyponatremia** in patients ≥65 years old within 2–4 weeks of drug initiation.
Long-Term Adherence
  • **Consistently Sustained Maintenance:** Compliance spanning at least **6 to 9 months** post-remission for a first unipolar episode, and up to **2 years** for recurrent diagnoses.
  • Evaluate compliance patterns non-judgmentally before executing any dosage shifts or treatment changes.
  • Proactively educate on the hazards of abrupt discontinuation syndrome (withdrawal features) and ensure the execution of a supervised, gradual taper schedule when stopping therapy.
Patient education
Education Element Patient Counseling Framework Teach-Back Verification Question
The Diagnosis Explain that depression is a treatable medical condition, not a personal weakness or a character flaw. Use the biological analogy of an "engine running out of fuel," where natural brain messengers (like serotonin) drop in efficiency, disrupting sleep, energy levels, physical processing, and mood.
"To make sure I explained this clearly, how would you describe what depression is to a close friend or family member?"
Purpose of Treatment Clarify that treatments (psychotherapy and/or medication) work gradually to restore standard neural pathways. The core goal is to steadily resolve symptoms, rebuild daily functional capacity, protect physical health, and prevent future recurrences of the condition.
"What is the main goal we are trying to achieve by combining your counseling sessions with this medication?"
Medication Adherence Emphasize that antidepressants usually require **4 to 6 weeks** of daily, uninterrupted use to fully improve mood. Instruct the patient never to stop taking the medication abruptly if they feel better or face early side effects, as sudden stoppage causes severe discontinuation symptoms.
"If you don't feel a change in your mood after taking the medicine for two weeks, what should you do instead of stopping it?"
Lifestyle Measures Focus on achievable behavioral targets: establish a rigid, dark sleep window to manage insomnia; engage in light, daily physical activity (like walking) to trigger endorphin response; completely limit alcohol and recreational drugs, which act as strong brain depressants and neutralize treatment.
"What are one or two daily lifestyle adjustments you feel ready to focus on this week to help support your recovery?"
Monitoring Requirements Explain that tracking sheets (like the PHQ-9 questionnaire) are used at regular checkups to objectively measure chemical shifts. Outline that regular appointments allow the clinician to catch unhelpful side effects early and safely optimize or adjust doses.
"When we meet for our follow-up checkup in two weeks, what specific elements of your health will we be tracking together?"
Urgent Care Red Flags Clearly define behavioral and mental indicators requiring immediate crisis support:
  • Emergence of active thoughts of self-harm, a desire to die, or formulating a specific suicide plan.
  • Sudden, paradoxical spikes in unmanageable panic, physical agitation, or restlessness.
  • The development of unusual manic energy, racing thoughts, or hearing or seeing things that aren't there.
"If you begin to feel an overwhelming urge to harm yourself or feel completely unsafe at home, what is your exact plan for getting immediate emergency help?"

Individualization
Individualization Vector Antidepressant Adjustments (SSRIs, SNRIs, TCAs) Mood Stabilizer & Augmentation Adjustments
Age & Pregnancy Status
  • Geriatric: Avoid TCAs entirely due to profound anticholinergic risks, orthostatic hypotension, and falls. Select SSRIs but monitor for hyponatremia.
  • Pregnancy: SSRIs (sertraline preferred) show favorable safety profiles. Avoid paroxetine due to cardiac defect links. Watch for neonatal adaptation syndrome.
  • Geriatric: Enforce lower initial dosing and tight serum therapeutic windows for Lithium due to reduced age-related neural tolerance.
  • Pregnancy: Lithium carries a risk of Ebstein’s anomaly (cardiac defect). Valproate is **strictly contraindicated** due to high risk of neural tube defects and low IQ outcomes.
Renal, Hepatic & Comorbid Burden
  • Hepatic/Renal Care: Most SSRIs require lower doses in hepatic failure (especially sertraline, fluoxetine). Duloxetine (SNRI) must be avoided in severe renal or chronic hepatic disease.
  • Cardiac: Avoid TCAs and high-dose citalopram/escitalopram in patients with long QTc intervals or severe heart disease.
  • Renal: Lithium is cleared exclusively by the kidneys; it is contraindicated or requires extreme dosage reductions in renal impairment.
  • Hepatic: Valproate and certain antipsychotics (olanzapine, clozapine) are highly hepatotoxic and require baseline/periodic liver function test screens.
Allergies & Polypharmacy
  • Serotonin Syndrome: Avoid combining SSRIs/SNRIs with MAOIs, linezolid, or tramadol.
  • Bleeding Risks: SSRIs impair platelet aggregation; use caution and monitor closely if co-prescribed with NSAIDs, aspirin, or oral anticoagulants (warfarin/DOACs).
  • Severe Allergies: Lamotrigine requires an extremely slow dose titration to avoid life-threatening Steven-Johnson Syndrome (SJS) rash.
  • Drug Interactions: Valproate doubles lamotrigine serum levels (increasing SJS rash risk)—cut lamotrigine titration doses by half if used together. Thiazide diuretics double lithium levels.
Socioeconomic Factors & Preferences
  • Cost Management: Prioritize affordable, older generation generic SSRIs (fluoxetine, sertraline) over expensive brand-name extended-release options.
  • Side Effect Matching: If a patient is concerned with weight gain or sexual dysfunction, avoid paroxetine and mirtazapine; select bupropion instead.
  • Lab Burden barriers: Lithium and Valproate require consistent blood draws for therapeutic level monitoring. If a patient lacks transit or insurance for labs, choose lamotrigine or a second-generation antipsychotic instead.
  • Address metabolic side effects (weight gain, dyslipidemia) of atypicals based on preference.

Patient Checklist

  • Know the name and purpose of each medicine.
  • Know how and when to take or use treatment.
  • Ask what adverse effects require medical attention.
  • Keep appointments and monitoring tests.
  • Tell the healthcare team about OTC medicines, supplements and allergies.
  • Do not stop long-term treatment abruptly without professional advice unless an emergency clinician instructs otherwise.

🚨 When urgent medical care may be needed

Suicidal intent/plan, psychosis, severe self-neglect or inability to maintain safety
