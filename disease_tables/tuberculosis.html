Clinical framework

What clinicians assess

This page organizes the major clinical decision points for Tuberculosis (TB). It is designed for patient education and professional revision, not as a substitute for a current guideline, local protocol or individualized clinical assessment.

Diagnosis / assessment
Diagnostic Modality Clinical Parameters & Testing Methods Diagnostic Interpretation & Actions
Rapid Molecular Testing (WRDs)
  • Xpert MTB/RIF / Ultra: Initial diagnostic test for sputum samples (detects M. tuberculosis DNA and Rifampicin resistance simultaneously).
  • Truenat MTB / MTB-RIF Dx: Point-of-care micro-PCR assay for rapid detection and rifampicin susceptibility testing.
  • Loop-Mediated Isothermal Amplification (TB-LAMP): Alternative rapid molecular assay for pulmonary TB detection in low-resource settings.
  • MTB Detected / Rif-Sensitive: Initiate standard 1st-line regimen immediately.
  • MTB Detected / Rif-Resistant: Promptly perform second-line drug susceptibility testing (Line Probe Assay / Next-Gen Sequencing) and start drug-resistant TB (DR-TB) regimen.
Microscopy & Culture Assays
  • Sputum Smear Microscopy: Ziehl-Neelsen (ZN) or LED Fluorescence microscopy for Acid-Fast Bacilli (AFB) detection (requires 2 spot samples).
  • Mycobacterial Culture: Liquid culture (MGIT) or solid culture (Lowenstein-Jensen / LJ media) to confirm diagnosis and conduct phenotypic Drug Susceptibility Testing (DST)[cite: 3].
  • Smear microscopy assesses baseline infectiousness and grading (1+, 2+, 3+).
  • Liquid culture is the gold standard for phenotypic DST against 1st and 2nd-line anti-TB drugs; required for paucibacillary or extrapulmonary samples[cite: 3].
Radiological Evaluation
  • Chest X-Ray (CXR): Screen for apical infiltrates, cavitary lesions, hilar lymphadenopathy, pleural effusion, or miliary pattern.
  • Computer-Aided Detection (CAD): AI-driven CXR screening software to flag high-probability TB cases for molecular confirmation.
  • CT Imaging / Ultrasound: Targeted imaging for Extrapulmonary TB (EPTB) (e.g., abdominal US, chest CT, brain MRI for TB meningitis).
  • Radiological findings suggest disease activity but require microbiological confirmation (Xpert or culture) where possible[cite: 3].
  • Useful in smear-negative / Xpert-negative cases with high clinical suspicion.
EPTB & Host Response Testing
  • Urine LF-LAM (Lipoarabinomannan): Antigen detection assay for immunocompromised patients (HIV-positive with low CD4 or severe illness).
  • Invasive Specimen Diagnostics: Xpert MTB/RIF on CSF, pleural fluid, lymph node aspirate, or tissue biopsy.
  • IGRA / TST (Mantoux): Interferon-Gamma Release Assays or Tuberculin Skin Test to detect latent TB infection (LTBI).
  • Positive Urine LF-LAM supports rapid treatment initiation in severe HIV/TB co-infection.
  • IGRA and TST measure immune response and confirm Latent TB Infection; they cannot distinguish active TB disease from latent infection.
Key monitoring
Monitoring Parameter Target Range & Monitoring Schedule Clinical Purpose & Action Thresholds
Sputum Microbiology & Smear Conversion
  • Sputum Smear & Culture: Perform at end of Month 2 (intensive phase end), Month 5, and Month 6 (treatment completion).
  • Target Outcome: Negative smear/culture conversion by end of Month 2.
  • Confirm treatment response and bacteriological clearance.
  • Persistent positive smear/culture at Month 2+ warrants repeat Xpert/DST for acquired drug resistance, treatment failure, or poor adherence[cite: 2].
Clinical Symptoms & Body Weight
  • Monthly Clinical Evaluation: Monitor cough resolution, fever defervescence, night sweats, and appetite improvement[cite: 2].
  • Body Weight: Check weight monthly; re-calculate drug doses if weight category changes[cite: 2].
  • Ensure clinical improvement and weight gain[cite: 2].
  • Under-dosing due to unadjusted weight gain can lead to therapeutic failure or drug resistance[cite: 2].
Hepatotoxicity & Laboratory Safety
  • Baseline & Serial LFTs: ALT, AST, Total Bilirubin at baseline, then monthly or bi-weekly if high risk (cirrhosis, HIV, alcohol misuse)[cite: 2].
  • Renal & Electrolyte Panel: Baseline Serum Creatinine and Urate[cite: 2].
  • Detect Drug-Induced Liver Injury (DILI) from Isoniazid, Rifampicin, or Pyrazinamide.
  • Action Threshold: Hold hepatotoxic drugs if ALT ≥ 5x ULN (asymptomatic) OR ALT ≥ 3x ULN with hepatitis symptoms (jaundice, nausea, abdominal pain).
Organ-Specific Adverse Effect Screening
  • Visual Acuity & Color Vision (Ethambutol): Ishihara testing & Snellen chart monthly.
  • Peripheral Neuropathy (Isoniazid): Regular clinical sensory screening.
  • Auditory/Vestibular (Injectables/Aminoglycosides): Baseline and periodic audiometry.
  • Detect optic neuritis early (stop Ethambutol immediately if color vision/acuity declines).
  • Administer prophylactic Pyridoxine (Vitamin B6 10–50 mg daily) to prevent INH-induced neuropathy[cite: 2].
Management principles
Management Category Standard Regimen & Dosing Framework Clinical Purpose & Indication Strategy
Drug-Susceptible TB (DS-TB)
  • Standard 6-Month Regimen (2HRZE / 4HR):
    • Intensive Phase (2 months): Rifampicin (R), Isoniazid (H), Pyrazinamide (Z), Ethambutol (E) daily.
    • Continuation Phase (4 months): Rifampicin (R) and Isoniazid (H) daily.
  • Fixed-Dose Combinations (FDCs): Dosed according to weight bands to optimize adherence[cite: 2].
  • First-line treatment for drug-susceptible pulmonary and extrapulmonary TB[cite: 2].
  • Intensive phase rapidly clears bacillary load; continuation phase eradicates persistent bacilli to prevent relapse[cite: 2].
Drug-Resistant TB (MDR / RR-TB)
  • All-Oral Short Regimens (BPaLM / BPaL): 6-month regimen comprising Bedaquiline (B), Pretomanid (P), Linezolid (L), and Moxifloxacin (M).
  • Longer Oral Regimens (18–20 months): Customized using Group A (Fluoroquinolones, Bedaquiline, Linezolid), Group B (Clofazimine, Cycloserine), and Group C agents based on DST.
  • Indicated for Rifampicin-Resistant (RR) or Multidrug-Resistant (MDR-TB)[cite: 2].
  • BPaLM is preferred by WHO due to higher cure rates and shorter duration without injectable agents.
Regimen Duration & Adjuvant Therapy
  • Extended Regimens: Continue H + R for 9–12 months in TB Meningitis, Bone/Joint TB, and Central Nervous System (CNS) lesions.
  • Adjuvant Corticosteroids: Dexamethasone or Prednisolone tapered over 6–8 weeks for TB Meningitis and TB Pericarditis.
  • Pyridoxine (Vitamin B6): 10–50 mg daily co-administered with Isoniazid.
  • Extended duration is required for slow-clearing or high-mortality organ sites.
  • Corticosteroids reduce mortality, severe neuro-deficits, and pericardial constriction.
  • Pyridoxine prevents peripheral neuropathy in high-risk populations (pregnant, HIV, malnourished)[cite: 2].
Treatment Delivery & Adherence Strategy
  • People-Centered Care / DOT / DAT: Directly Observed Therapy (DOT) or Digital Adherence Technologies (video DOT, smart pillboxes).
  • Infection Control: Respiratory isolation, negative pressure rooms (if available), and well-ventilated spaces until smear-negative.
  • Ensures strict medication adherence to prevent acquired drug resistance[cite: 2].
  • Minimizes community and nosocomial transmission of Mycobacterium tuberculosis.
Follow-up / outcomes
Outcome Domain Evaluation Parameters & Schedule Target Outcomes & Action Thresholds
Adherence & Bacteriological Cure
  • Sputum Smear & Culture Evaluation: Assess at Month 2 (intensive phase end), Month 5, and Month 6 (treatment end).
  • Dose Tracking: Measure pill counts, electronic adherence logs (DAT), or DOT records monthly.
  • Target: Complete treatment with documented negative sputum conversion ("Cured" status).
  • Action Threshold: Positive smear at Month 2+ or missed doses (> 2 weeks) requires immediate DST re-assessment and adherence counseling[cite: 2].
Clinical Improvement & Nutritional Recovery
  • Symptom Review: Confirm resolution of fever, night sweats, hemoptysis, and cough[cite: 2].
  • Weight & BMI Tracking: Record body weight monthly to guide drug dosage adjustments[cite: 2].
  • Target: Complete clinical symptom resolution and steady weight gain.
  • Action: Lack of weight gain or lingering systemic symptoms requires evaluation for malabsorption, drug resistance, HIV co-infection, or non-TB pathology[cite: 2].
Adverse Event & Toxicity Clearance
  • Hepatic Function (ALT/AST/Bilirubin): Routine LFTs monthly or upon symptom onset (nausea, vomiting, jaundice)[cite: 2].
  • Visual & Auditory Checks: Assess color vision/visual acuity (Ethambutol) and audiometry (Linezolid/Injectables)[cite: 2].
  • Target: Normal LFTs and absence of permanent drug-induced organ impairment.
  • Action: Reintroduce anti-TB drugs sequentially once LFTs return to < 2x ULN following DILI; stop Ethambutol permanently if optic neuritis occurs.
Post-TB Sequelae & Relapse Screening
  • End-of-Treatment CXR: Establish a baseline post-cure radiograph at Month 6.
  • Post-TB Lung Disease (PTLD): Evaluate residual breathlessness and spirometry at 6 and 12 months post-cure.
  • Relapse Surveillance: Monitor for recurring symptoms for 6–12 months post-treatment completion.
  • Target: Zero relapse and early identification of chronic lung damage (bronchiectasis, fibrotic scarring).
  • Action: Pulmonary rehabilitation for PTLD; immediate sputum Xpert MTB/RIF for any recurrent respiratory symptoms.
Patient education
Education Domain Core Clinical Explanation & Guidance Teach-Back Prompts & Verification
Diagnosis & Purpose of Treatment
  • Understanding TB: Reassure the patient that Tuberculosis is a curable bacterial infection spread through the air when coughing or sneezing.
  • Full Course Necessity: Explain that treatment takes a minimum of 6 months. Stopping early, even if feeling better, can cause the bacteria to return stronger and become resistant to standard drugs.
  • Orange Fluids: Reassure them that Rifampicin safely turns urine, sweat, and tears an orange-red color[cite: 2].
  • "In your own words, why is it necessary to finish all 6 months of your medication even after your cough goes away?"[cite: 2]
  • "What change in the color of your urine should you expect while taking your daily pills?"[cite: 2]
Medication Adherence & Safety
  • Daily Routine: Emphasize taking all prescribed tablets once daily on an empty stomach (1 hour before or 2 hours after meals) with water[cite: 2].
  • Managing Side Effects: Teach the patient that mild nausea is common, but severe stomach pain, yellow eyes (jaundice), or skin rash require urgent medical review[cite: 2].
  • Vitamin B6: Explain that Pyridoxine is taken alongside TB drugs to prevent hand and foot tingling (numbness)[cite: 2].
  • "What time of day will you take your TB pills, and should you take them with food or on an empty stomach?"[cite: 2]
  • "What will you do if you notice your eyes turning yellow or experience severe vomiting?"[cite: 2]
Lifestyle Measures & Infection Control
  • Cough Hygiene: Instruct on covering the mouth/nose when coughing or wearing a surgical mask during the first 2–3 weeks of therapy[cite: 2].
  • Home Ventilation: Advise keeping living spaces well-ventilated with open windows and doors to let air and sunlight in[cite: 2].
  • Nutrition & Substance Avoidance: Encourage a high-protein diet to rebuild strength; strictly advise against alcohol intake as it increases liver injury risk[cite: 2].
  • "What steps can you take at home during the first few weeks to protect your family from catching TB?"[cite: 2]
  • "Why is it essential to completely avoid alcohol while you are on TB treatment?"[cite: 2]
Monitoring & When to Seek Urgent Care
  • Follow-up Visits: Remind the patient to return monthly for sputum tests, weight checks, and pill refills[cite: 2].
  • Contact Tracing: Encourage household family members (especially children under 5 and immunocompromised individuals) to get screened for TB[cite: 2].
  • Urgent Red Flags:
    • Coughing up blood (hemoptysis)[cite: 2]
    • Yellowing of eyes/skin (jaundice)[cite: 2]
    • Blurry or altered color vision (Ethambutol effect)[cite: 2]
    • Severe dizziness or hearing loss[cite: 2]
  • "If you notice changes in your eyesight or start having trouble seeing colors, what should you do right away?"[cite: 2]
  • "When is your next scheduled clinic appointment for your sputum check and medication pickup?"[cite: 2]
Multidisciplinary roles
Healthcare Discipline Core Responsibilities & Interventions Collaborative Synergy & Clinical Goals
Physicians / Pulmonologists / Infectious Disease
  • Diagnosis & Regimen Design: Confirm diagnosis using molecular and culture diagnostics; prescribe standard or MDR-TB regimens.
  • Complex Case Management: Manage extrapulmonary manifestations, HIV co-infection, and severe adverse drug reactions (e.g., DILI).
  • Leads clinical decision-making and oversees treatment adjustments in response to DST results and clinical response.
TB Nurses & Public Health Officers
  • Direct Care & DOT/DAT Facilitation: Coordinate daily medication delivery, digital adherence tracking, and symptom monitoring[cite: 2].
  • Contact Tracing & Screening: Conduct household investigations, contact tracing, and preventive therapy (TPT) administration[cite: 2].
  • Serves as the primary bridge between clinical care and the patient's home environment to maintain retention in care[cite: 2].
Clinical Pharmacists
  • Dosing & Drug Interactions: Review weight-band dosing, evaluate drug-drug interactions (especially with antiretrovirals), and manage supply chains[cite: 2].
  • Toxicity Monitoring: Monitor therapeutic drug levels when indicated and counsel patients on potential adverse effects[cite: 2].
  • Ensures uninterrupted medication supply, prevents drug resistance due to inappropriate dosing, and optimizes drug tolerance[cite: 2].
Community Health Workers & Social Care
  • Social Support: Provide nutritional support packages, transport subsidies, and psycho-social counseling[cite: 2].
  • Stigma Reduction: Deliver community education and support patient support groups to reduce TB stigma[cite: 2].
  • Addresses socio-economic barriers to care, improving treatment completion rates and reducing loss to follow-up[cite: 2].
Individualization
Individualization Variable Clinical Assessment & Risk Modifiers Tailored Management Adjustments
Age Extremes (Pediatric & Elderly)
  • Pediatrics (< 5 yrs): High risk for rapid disease progression, severe disseminated TB, and paucibacillary sputum samples.
  • Elderly (≥ 65 yrs): Increased baseline risk of drug-induced liver injury (DILI) and atypical radiological presentations.
  • Use pediatric dispersible FDC formulations; adjust dosing by weight at every visit.
  • Perform closer baseline and serial LFT monitoring in older adults; maintain vigilance for pill burden and polypharmacy.
Pregnancy & Lactation
  • Untreated TB Risk: Active TB carries significant risk of perinatal mortality, low birth weight, and premature delivery.
  • Drug Safety: Standard 1st-line drugs (H, R, E, Z) cross the placenta but are considered safe[cite: 2]. Aminoglycosides are ototoxic to the fetus.
  • Treat drug-susceptible TB immediately with standard HRZE regimen; do not delay therapy[cite: 2].
  • Co-administer Pyridoxine (Vitamin B6 25–50 mg daily) to prevent peripheral neuropathy in mother and fetus[cite: 2]. Breastfeeding is safe.
Renal & Hepatic Impairment
  • Severe Renal Impairment (CrCl < 30 mL/min): Pyrazinamide and Ethambutol clearance is reduced, leading to toxicity risk.
  • Pre-existing Hepatic Disease: Baseline liver dysfunction elevates risk of acute liver injury from INH, RIF, and PZA[cite: 2].
  • Renal: Reduce Pyrazinamide and Ethambutol dosing frequency to 3 times weekly (post-dialysis on hemodialysis days).
  • Hepatic: In advanced liver disease, consider non-hepatotoxic or hepatoprotective sparing regimens (e.g., Ethambutol + Levofloxacin + Streptomycin).
Comorbidities & Drug Interactions
  • HIV Co-infection: Risk of Immune Reconstitution Inflammatory Syndrome (IRIS) and severe drug interactions via Rifampicin (CYP430 inducer)[cite: 2].
  • Diabetes Mellitus: Hyperglycemia reduces anti-TB drug exposure and increases treatment failure/relapse risk.
  • Start ART within 2 weeks of TB treatment initiation for CD4 < 50 (or within 8 weeks for CD4 ≥ 50). Substitute Rifabutin for Rifampicin if taking protease inhibitors.
  • Optimize glycemic control with regular HbA1c monitoring.
Socioeconomic Barriers & Preferences
  • Access & Financial Hardship: Travel costs, catastrophic health expenditure, and job insecurity affecting treatment completion[cite: 2].
  • Stigma & Operational Constraints: Fear of disclosure at workplace or home affecting daily clinic visits[cite: 2].
  • Offer Digital Adherence Technologies (DAT) or community-based DOT as flexible alternatives to daily facility visits[cite: 2].
  • Connect patients with nutritional packages, travel subsidies, and psycho-social support networks[cite: 2].

Patient Checklist

  • Know the name and purpose of each medicine.
  • Know how and when to take or use treatment.
  • Ask what adverse effects require medical attention.
  • Keep appointments and monitoring tests.
  • Tell the healthcare team about OTC medicines, supplements and allergies.
  • Do not stop long-term treatment abruptly without professional advice unless an emergency clinician instructs otherwise.

🚨 When urgent medical care may be needed

Severe drug reaction, jaundice, visual disturbance, severe neurologic symptoms
