Comprehensive Cancer Clinical Reference

Cancer management requires an individualized approach based on the anatomical site, histological type, stage, molecular characteristics, performance status, organ function, comorbidities and patient preferences.

The clinical tables below provide an expanded overview of assessment, diagnosis, laboratory monitoring, imaging, treatment principles, commonly used treatment combinations, adverse-effect monitoring, follow-up, multidisciplinary roles and patient-specific considerations.

Clinical reminder:

Cancer treatment regimens vary according to stage, histology, biomarkers, treatment intent and local oncology protocols. The combinations listed below are examples of established treatment approaches and should not be regarded as individual prescriptions.

01

Breast Cancer

Malignancy arising from breast tissue. Treatment is strongly influenced by tumour stage, histology, hormone-receptor status and HER2 status.

Clinical Domain Detailed Clinical Information
Clinical Assessment
  • History of breast lump, pain or breast enlargement.
  • Changes in breast shape or size.
  • Nipple inversion or abnormal discharge.
  • Skin dimpling, tethering, ulceration or peau d'orange.
  • Axillary or supraclavicular lymph-node enlargement.
  • Previous breast cancer or family history.
  • Previous exposure to chest irradiation.
  • Reproductive and hormonal history.
Physical examination:
  • Inspection of both breasts.
  • Palpation of breast tissue.
  • Assessment of axillary and supraclavicular nodes.
  • Assessment for skin and nipple abnormalities.
Diagnosis & Pathology Imaging:
  • Diagnostic mammography.
  • Breast ultrasound.
  • MRI in selected clinical situations.
Definitive diagnosis:

Suspicious lesions generally require tissue diagnosis, commonly using a core needle biopsy.

Pathology should establish:
  • Histological type.
  • Tumour grade.
  • Estrogen receptor (ER).
  • Progesterone receptor (PR).
  • HER2 status.
  • Additional biomarkers when clinically indicated.
Staging Staging evaluates:
  • Primary tumour size.
  • Regional lymph-node involvement.
  • Distant metastasis.

Stage, grade and tumour biology determine treatment strategy and prognosis.

Laboratory Monitoring
  • FBC: anaemia, neutropenia and thrombocytopenia.
  • Renal function: creatinine/eGFR where relevant.
  • LFTs: particularly during systemic therapy.
  • Electrolytes: according to treatment.
  • Pregnancy testing: where applicable.
  • Cardiac function: particularly with HER2-directed therapy and anthracyclines.
Management Principles Treatment depends on stage and biological subtype.

Local Treatment

  • Breast-conserving surgery in suitable patients.
  • Mastectomy where indicated.
  • Appropriate axillary management.
  • Radiotherapy when indicated.

Systemic Treatment

  • Chemotherapy.
  • Endocrine therapy for hormone-receptor-positive disease.
  • HER2-directed therapy for HER2-positive disease.
  • CDK4/6 inhibitors in selected HR-positive/HER2-negative disease.
  • Immunotherapy in selected triple-negative disease.
  • Other biomarker-directed therapies when indicated.

Examples of Combination Approaches

  • HER2-positive disease: chemotherapy + trastuzumab-based treatment; pertuzumab may be incorporated in selected settings.
  • HR-positive/HER2-negative advanced disease: endocrine therapy + CDK4/6 inhibitor.
  • Selected triple-negative disease: chemotherapy + immunotherapy.
Major Toxicities to Monitor
  • Myelosuppression.
  • Peripheral neuropathy with taxanes.
  • Cardiotoxicity with anthracyclines.
  • Cardiac dysfunction with HER2-directed therapy.
  • Thromboembolic risk with tamoxifen.
  • Bone loss with aromatase inhibitors.
  • Immune-mediated toxicities with immunotherapy.
Follow-Up & Outcomes Follow-up should assess:
  • Local recurrence.
  • Regional recurrence.
  • Distant recurrence.
  • Treatment toxicity.
  • Adherence to endocrine or oral systemic therapy.
  • Bone health where relevant.
  • Psychological and social wellbeing.
Mammographic surveillance is an important component of follow-up.
Individualization & Comorbidities Consider:
  • Cardiac disease: important with anthracyclines and HER2 therapy.
  • Renal impairment: may affect selected medicines.
  • Hepatic disease: may alter drug metabolism.
  • Diabetes: corticosteroids may worsen glycaemic control.
  • History of thrombosis: relevant when considering tamoxifen.
  • Osteoporosis: important with aromatase inhibitors.
  • Pregnancy: major implications for treatment selection.
Multidisciplinary Roles
Oncologist

Coordinates systemic cancer treatment.

Breast Surgeon

Performs surgical management and appropriate axillary procedures.

Radiation Oncologist

Plans and administers radiotherapy.

Pathologist

Provides histological and biomarker characterization.

Pharmacist

Reviews medicines, interactions, dosing, toxicity and adherence.

Nurse

Provides treatment administration, monitoring and education.
02

Prostate Cancer

Cancer arising from prostate tissue. Assessment and treatment depend on PSA, biopsy findings, Grade Group, stage and risk classification.

Clinical Domain Detailed Clinical Information
Clinical Assessment
  • Lower urinary tract symptoms.
  • Urinary frequency and nocturia.
  • Weak urinary stream.
  • Difficulty initiating urination.
  • Haematuria.
  • Erectile or sexual dysfunction.
  • Bone pain in advanced disease.
  • Family history and hereditary cancer risk.
Diagnosis
  • Serum PSA assessment.
  • Digital rectal examination where indicated.
  • Multiparametric prostate MRI.
  • Targeted and/or systematic prostate biopsy.
Pathology:
  • Gleason score.
  • Grade Group.
  • Tumour extent.
Laboratory Monitoring
  • PSA: response and progression monitoring.
  • Testosterone: particularly during ADT when clinically indicated.
  • FBC: during chemotherapy.
  • LFTs: with relevant systemic therapies.
  • Creatinine/eGFR.
  • Potassium: particularly with abiraterone.
  • Blood pressure: important with abiraterone and some androgen-pathway treatments.
Management Principles

Localized Disease

  • Active surveillance for selected low-risk disease.
  • Radical prostatectomy.
  • External-beam radiotherapy.
  • Brachytherapy in selected patients.

Advanced / Metastatic Disease

Androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) is a major treatment backbone.

  • ADT + abiraterone + corticosteroid.
  • ADT + enzalutamide.
  • ADT + apalutamide.
  • ADT + docetaxel in selected patients.
  • Combination intensification in selected high-volume/high-risk disease.
Major Toxicities
  • Hot flashes.
  • Reduced libido.
  • Erectile dysfunction.
  • Metabolic changes.
  • Bone mineral loss.
  • Cardiovascular risk.
  • Fatigue.
  • Abiraterone-associated hypertension, hypokalaemia and fluid retention.
  • Docetaxel-associated neutropenia and neuropathy.
Follow-Up & Outcomes
  • PSA trend.
  • Testosterone where appropriate.
  • Urinary symptoms.
  • Sexual function.
  • Bone health.
  • Metabolic and cardiovascular health.
  • Evidence of metastatic progression.
Individualization & Comorbidities
  • Cardiovascular disease: important with prolonged ADT.
  • Hypertension: particularly important with abiraterone.
  • Hypokalaemia: may complicate abiraterone therapy.
  • Osteoporosis: ADT can accelerate bone loss.
  • Diabetes: monitor metabolic effects.
  • Renal impairment: consider treatment and supportive-drug requirements.
  • Hepatic impairment: consider when selecting systemic therapy.
  • Frailty: treatment intensity may require adjustment.
Multidisciplinary Roles
Urologist

Diagnosis and surgical/local management.

Oncologist

Systemic treatment and disease monitoring.

Radiation Oncologist

Radiotherapy and brachytherapy.

Pharmacist

Medication optimization, interactions and adherence.

Nurse

Education, monitoring and treatment support.

Dietitian / Physiotherapist

Metabolic, nutritional and functional support.
03

Cervical Cancer

Most cervical cancers are associated with persistent high-risk HPV infection. Prevention, screening and early detection are major components of cervical cancer control.

Clinical Domain Detailed Clinical Information
Clinical Assessment
  • Post-coital bleeding.
  • Intermenstrual bleeding.
  • Postmenopausal bleeding.
  • Abnormal vaginal discharge.
  • Pelvic pain.
  • Dyspareunia.
  • Urinary or bowel symptoms in advanced disease.
Screening & Diagnosis Screening may involve:
  • HPV testing.
  • Cervical cytology where appropriate.
  • Visual inspection approaches where applicable.
Abnormal results may require:
  • Colposcopy.
  • Directed biopsy.
  • Histopathological examination.
Staging Evaluate:
  • Primary cervical tumour.
  • Parametrial involvement.
  • Pelvic/para-aortic lymph nodes.
  • Bladder or bowel involvement where relevant.
  • Distant metastasis.
Imaging may include MRI, CT or PET/CT depending on disease stage and availability.
Laboratory Monitoring
  • FBC: chemotherapy-related cytopenias.
  • Creatinine/eGFR: particularly with cisplatin.
  • Magnesium: cisplatin-associated electrolyte loss.
  • Potassium: electrolyte monitoring.
  • LFTs: systemic treatment monitoring.
  • Pregnancy testing: when applicable.
Management Principles

Early Disease

  • Appropriate surgical management.
  • Radical hysterectomy in selected patients.
  • Fertility-sparing procedures in carefully selected early disease.

Locally Advanced Disease

Concurrent chemoradiotherapy is a major treatment approach.

Common radiosensitizing regimen:

Cisplatin + external-beam radiotherapy with brachytherapy as appropriate.

Recurrent / Advanced Disease

  • Platinum-based systemic therapy.
  • Taxane-containing combinations.
  • Bevacizumab in selected patients.
  • Immunotherapy in selected biomarker-defined settings.
Major Toxicities
  • Cisplatin nephrotoxicity.
  • Hypomagnesaemia.
  • Peripheral neuropathy.
  • Myelosuppression.
  • Nausea and vomiting.
  • Radiation-related gastrointestinal and genitourinary toxicity.
  • Infertility and reproductive consequences depending on treatment.
Follow-Up & Outcomes Follow-up evaluates:
  • Local recurrence.
  • Pelvic recurrence.
  • Distant metastasis.
  • Radiation complications.
  • Sexual and reproductive health.
  • Psychological wellbeing.
  • Nutritional status.
Individualization & Comorbidities
  • Renal impairment: major consideration before cisplatin.
  • Hearing impairment: relevant because of platinum toxicity.
  • Neuropathy: may influence platinum/taxane therapy.
  • Pregnancy: requires specialist management.
  • Fertility: discuss preservation before treatment where appropriate.
  • HIV/immunosuppression: coordinate cancer and infectious-disease management.
  • Malnutrition: nutritional intervention may be required.
Multidisciplinary Roles
Gynaecologist / Gynaecologic Oncologist

Diagnosis, staging and surgical management.

Medical Oncologist

Systemic treatment.

Radiation Oncologist

External radiation and brachytherapy.

Pathologist

Histological diagnosis.

Pharmacist

Chemotherapy verification, interactions, supportive therapy and adherence.

Nurse

Treatment administration, monitoring and patient education.
04

Throat / Head & Neck Cancer

Includes cancers arising from structures such as the pharynx, larynx, oral cavity and oropharynx. Treatment depends heavily on the anatomical subsite and stage.

Clinical Domain Detailed Clinical Information
Clinical Assessment
  • Persistent sore throat.
  • Persistent hoarseness.
  • Dysphagia.
  • Odynophagia.
  • Persistent mouth or throat ulcer.
  • Neck mass.
  • Unexplained ear pain.
  • Weight loss.
  • Difficulty breathing.
Assess smoking, alcohol exposure and relevant HPV-associated disease risk.
Diagnosis
  • Complete oral and head-and-neck examination.
  • Palpation of cervical lymph nodes.
  • Flexible nasoendoscopy or direct endoscopy.
  • Biopsy of suspicious lesions.
  • Fine-needle aspiration/core biopsy of appropriate neck masses.
Imaging may include:
  • CT.
  • MRI.
  • PET/CT in selected cases.
HPV testing is particularly relevant to appropriate oropharyngeal cancers.
Laboratory Monitoring
  • FBC.
  • Renal function.
  • Liver function.
  • Electrolytes.
  • Magnesium during cisplatin therapy.
  • Albumin/nutritional assessment where appropriate.
Management Principles

Early Disease

  • Surgery.
  • Radiotherapy.

Locally Advanced Disease

Surgery followed by appropriate adjuvant therapy or organ-preserving chemoradiotherapy may be considered depending on site and stage.

Common radiosensitizing approach:

Cisplatin + radiotherapy in suitable patients.

Advanced Disease

  • Platinum + fluoropyrimidine combinations.
  • Platinum + taxane combinations.
  • Cetuximab-containing therapy in selected patients.
  • PD-1-directed immunotherapy in selected advanced disease.
Key Toxicities
  • Oral mucositis.
  • Xerostomia.
  • Dysphagia.
  • Malnutrition.
  • Cisplatin nephrotoxicity.
  • Peripheral neuropathy.
  • Myelosuppression.
  • Radiation dermatitis.
Follow-Up & Outcomes
  • Head-and-neck examination.
  • Neck lymph-node examination.
  • Endoscopic assessment when appropriate.
  • Speech and swallowing assessment.
  • Nutritional assessment.
  • Dental and oral-health assessment.
  • Assessment for recurrence.
Individualization & Comorbidities
  • Renal impairment: important before cisplatin.
  • Hearing impairment: platinum therapy consideration.
  • Neuropathy: may worsen with neurotoxic treatment.
  • Dental disease: should be addressed before radiotherapy where possible.
  • Malnutrition: aggressive nutritional support may be necessary.
  • Airway compromise: requires urgent multidisciplinary assessment.
  • Smoking/alcohol use: cessation support should be incorporated.
Multidisciplinary Roles
ENT / Head & Neck Surgeon

Diagnosis and surgical management.

Medical Oncologist

Systemic treatment.

Radiation Oncologist

Radiotherapy.

Dentist

Oral-health assessment and management.

Speech & Language Therapist

Speech and swallowing rehabilitation.

Dietitian

Nutritional assessment and support.

Pharmacist

Medication optimization, interactions and toxicity management.

Nurse

Administration, monitoring and education.
05

Lung Cancer

Lung cancer is broadly divided into non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small-cell lung cancer (SCLC). Molecular characterization is particularly important in advanced NSCLC.

Clinical Domain Detailed Clinical Information
Clinical Assessment
  • Persistent cough.
  • Haemoptysis.
  • Chest pain.
  • Shortness of breath.
  • Recurrent chest infections.
  • Hoarseness.
  • Unexplained weight loss.
  • Fatigue.
Assess:
  • Smoking history.
  • Occupational exposures.
  • Performance status.
  • Existing COPD or interstitial lung disease.
Diagnosis & Histology Initial imaging:
  • Chest X-ray where appropriate.
  • Contrast-enhanced CT chest.
Confirmation: Tissue biopsy.

Major categories:
  • Non-small-cell lung cancer.
  • Small-cell lung cancer.
  • Adenocarcinoma.
  • Squamous cell carcinoma.
  • Other histological subtypes.
Molecular Assessment In appropriate advanced NSCLC, molecular testing may include:
  • EGFR
  • ALK
  • ROS1
  • BRAF
  • KRAS
  • MET
  • RET
  • NTRK
  • HER2
  • PD-L1

Molecular results can determine whether targeted treatment is appropriate.

Laboratory Monitoring
  • FBC.
  • Renal function.
  • Liver function.
  • Electrolytes.
  • LDH when clinically appropriate.
  • Thyroid function during immunotherapy when indicated.
  • Adrenal/endocrine assessment when clinically indicated.
Management Principles

Early NSCLC

  • Surgical resection where operable.
  • Adjuvant chemotherapy in selected patients.
  • Adjuvant targeted therapy for selected molecular subtypes.
  • Adjuvant immunotherapy in selected patients.

Advanced NSCLC

Molecular testing should guide treatment whenever clinically appropriate.

  • EGFR alteration → EGFR-directed therapy.
  • ALK alteration → ALK inhibitor.
  • ROS1 alteration → ROS1-directed therapy.
  • BRAF V600E → BRAF/MEK targeted therapy.
  • RET alteration → RET inhibitor.
  • MET exon 14 alteration → MET-directed therapy.
  • KRAS G12C → KRAS-directed therapy.
  • NTRK fusion → TRK inhibitor.
  • Selected patients → immunotherapy ± platinum-based chemotherapy.

Small-Cell Lung Cancer

Platinum-based chemotherapy combined with etoposide is a major systemic treatment backbone, with immunotherapy incorporated in appropriate extensive-stage treatment protocols.

Major Toxicities
  • Myelosuppression.
  • Nephrotoxicity with platinum agents.
  • Peripheral neuropathy.
  • Nausea and vomiting.
  • Hepatotoxicity.
  • Immune-mediated pneumonitis.
  • Immune-mediated hepatitis.
  • Endocrine immune-related adverse effects.
Follow-Up & Outcomes
  • Clinical respiratory assessment.
  • CT-based response assessment where indicated.
  • Assessment for recurrence/progression.
  • Performance status.
  • Long-term treatment toxicity.
  • Smoking cessation and relapse prevention.
Individualization & Comorbidities
  • COPD: important for respiratory reserve.
  • Interstitial lung disease: relevant to pneumonitis risk.
  • Renal impairment: affects platinum-based treatment.
  • Hepatic disease: may influence systemic therapy.
  • Cardiovascular disease: important for overall treatment selection.
  • Autoimmune disease: important before immunotherapy.
  • Performance status: strongly influences treatment intensity.
Multidisciplinary Roles
Pulmonologist

Respiratory assessment and diagnostic procedures.

Thoracic Surgeon

Surgical management.

Medical Oncologist

Systemic treatment.

Radiation Oncologist

Radiation treatment.

Pathologist / Molecular Laboratory

Histology and molecular characterization.

Pharmacist

Systemic medicine optimization and toxicity monitoring.

Nurse

Monitoring, education and treatment support.
06

Melanoma

A malignant tumour arising from melanocytes. Early diagnosis and appropriate surgical management are particularly important.

Clinical Domain Detailed Clinical Information
Clinical Assessment ABCDE approach:
  • A – Asymmetry.
  • B – Border irregularity.
  • C – Colour variation.
  • D – Diameter/change in size.
  • E – Evolution.
Other concerning findings:
  • Bleeding.
  • Ulceration.
  • Persistent itching.
  • Rapid lesion change.
Diagnosis & Pathology

Suspicious lesions require appropriate biopsy and histopathological examination.

Important pathological factors include:
  • Breslow thickness.
  • Ulceration.
  • Margins.
  • Other pathological staging features.
Laboratory & Molecular Monitoring
  • FBC where systemic therapy requires it.
  • LFTs.
  • Renal function.
  • LDH in advanced disease where clinically relevant.
  • Thyroid function during immune checkpoint therapy.
  • Cortisol/adrenal assessment where clinically indicated.
Molecular testing: BRAF testing is important in appropriate advanced melanoma because BRAF-targeted treatment may be available.
Management Principles

Localized Disease

  • Wide local excision according to tumour characteristics.
  • Sentinel lymph-node assessment when indicated.

Advanced Disease

Immune checkpoint inhibitors are major systemic treatment options.

  • Pembrolizumab.
  • Nivolumab.
  • Nivolumab + ipilimumab.
  • Nivolumab + relatlimab.

BRAF V600-Positive Disease

  • Dabrafenib + trametinib.
  • Encorafenib + binimetinib.
  • Vemurafenib + cobimetinib.
Major Toxicities Immunotherapy:
  • Dermatitis.
  • Colitis.
  • Hepatitis.
  • Pneumonitis.
  • Thyroid dysfunction.
  • Hypophysitis.
  • Adrenal insufficiency.
  • Nephritis.
BRAF/MEK therapy:
  • Pyrexia.
  • Skin reactions.
  • Ocular toxicity.
  • Cardiac effects.
Follow-Up
  • Complete skin examination.
  • Regional lymph-node examination.
  • Assessment for recurrence.
  • Assessment for new primary melanomas.
  • Monitoring for late treatment toxicity.
Patients should practise ongoing skin awareness.
Individualization & Comorbidities
  • Autoimmune disease: important before immunotherapy.
  • Organ transplantation: immune checkpoint therapy requires specialist consideration.
  • Hepatic disease: important with systemic treatment.
  • Renal impairment: consider for supportive medicines.
  • Cardiac disease: relevant to selected targeted therapies.
Multidisciplinary Roles
Dermatologist

Skin assessment and diagnosis.

Surgeon

Excision and lymph-node management.

Oncologist

Systemic treatment.

Pathologist

Histology and tumour staging.

Pharmacist

Targeted/immunotherapy monitoring and medicine counselling.

Nurse

Education and adverse-effect monitoring.
07

Lymphoma

Lymphoma is broadly classified into Hodgkin lymphoma and non-Hodgkin lymphoma. Treatment varies substantially according to histological and molecular subtype.

Clinical Domain Detailed Clinical Information
Clinical Assessment
  • Painless lymph-node enlargement.
  • Fever.
  • Drenching night sweats.
  • Unexplained weight loss.
  • Fatigue.
  • Pruritus.
  • Abdominal fullness.
  • Recurrent infections.
Assess:
  • Peripheral lymph nodes.
  • Abdominal organs.
  • Performance status.
  • B symptoms.
Diagnosis & Classification Definitive diagnosis:

Tissue biopsy is generally required. Excisional lymph-node biopsy is preferred where feasible because tissue architecture is important.

Laboratory/pathology characterization:
  • Histopathology.
  • Immunohistochemistry.
  • Flow cytometry where appropriate.
  • Cytogenetic testing.
  • Molecular testing where indicated.
Staging Depending on lymphoma subtype:
  • CT.
  • PET/CT.
  • Bone marrow examination in selected cases.
  • LDH.
  • Performance status assessment.
Laboratory Monitoring
  • FBC: myelosuppression.
  • LDH: disease burden/prognostic assessment where appropriate.
  • Uric acid: tumour lysis risk.
  • Potassium.
  • Phosphate.
  • Calcium.
  • Creatinine.
  • LFTs.
Management Principles

Classical Hodgkin Lymphoma

Combination chemotherapy is used, with regimen selection depending on stage and current protocol.

Important regimen:

ABVD = doxorubicin + bleomycin + vinblastine + dacarbazine.

Contemporary treatment may incorporate brentuximab vedotin or nivolumab in selected patients.

Aggressive B-cell Non-Hodgkin Lymphoma

Common chemoimmunotherapy backbone:

R-CHOP = rituximab + cyclophosphamide + doxorubicin + vincristine + prednisone.

Treatment varies according to lymphoma subtype and risk.

Other Lymphoma Subtypes

Different subtypes may require substantially different approaches, including:

  • R-CVP in selected indolent B-cell lymphomas.
  • More intensive regimens for selected aggressive lymphomas.
  • Salvage chemoimmunotherapy.
  • Stem-cell transplantation in selected relapsed/refractory disease.
  • Targeted and cellular therapies in selected settings.
Major Toxicities
  • Myelosuppression.
  • Febrile neutropenia.
  • Peripheral neuropathy.
  • Anthracycline cardiotoxicity.
  • Bleomycin pulmonary toxicity.
  • Tumour lysis syndrome.
  • Rituximab infusion reactions.
  • Hepatitis B reactivation risk with rituximab-based treatment.
Follow-Up & Outcomes Follow-up evaluates:
  • Remission status.
  • Relapse.
  • Residual lymphadenopathy.
  • Treatment toxicity.
  • Secondary malignancies.
  • Cardiac complications.
  • Pulmonary complications.
  • Endocrine and fertility effects where relevant.
Individualization & Comorbidities
  • Cardiac disease: important with anthracyclines.
  • Pulmonary disease: particularly relevant with bleomycin.
  • Renal impairment: affects supportive and anticancer medicines.
  • Hepatic impairment: may require treatment modification.
  • Baseline neuropathy: important with vincristine.
  • Hepatitis B: screen and manage appropriately before rituximab-based therapy.
  • High tumour burden: increases tumour lysis risk.
  • Frailty: may require modification of treatment intensity.

Diagnosis, classification and treatment.

Lymphoma classification and molecular characterization.

Radiologist / Nuclear Medicine

Chemotherapy verification, supportive care, interactions and toxicity monitoring.

Nurse

Administration, monitoring and education.

Patient Education Across Cancer Care

Medication Adherence

Take medicines exactly as instructed. Do not double doses or discontinue anticancer treatment without contacting the healthcare team.

Drug Interactions

Tell the pharmacist and oncology team about all prescription medicines, OTC medicines, herbal products, supplements and traditional medicines.

Monitoring

Attend scheduled blood tests, imaging, oncology reviews and other monitoring appointments.

Urgent Symptoms

Seek urgent medical attention for fever, severe breathlessness, chest pain, uncontrolled bleeding, severe allergic reactions, confusion, seizures or rapidly worsening symptoms.

Healthy Lifestyle

Where medically appropriate, maintain physical activity, adequate nutrition, healthy weight, tobacco avoidance and appropriate alcohol reduction.

Teach-Back

Patients should be encouraged to explain their treatment plan in their own words so that misunderstandings can be identified and corrected.

Important Clinical Note

Cancer treatment is highly individualized. Treatment combinations listed in this reference are examples of established clinical approaches and are not prescriptions. Actual treatment selection, dose, treatment sequence and duration depend on cancer subtype, stage, biomarkers, performance status, organ function, previous therapy, treatment intent, local protocols and medicine availability.

Patients should discuss treatment decisions with their oncology team. Pharmacists contribute through medication reconciliation, dose optimization, interaction assessment, adverse-effect monitoring, supportive-care optimization and adherence counselling.

Patient Checklist

  • Know the name and purpose of each medicine.
  • Know how and when to take or use treatment.
  • Ask what adverse effects require medical attention.
  • Keep appointments and monitoring tests.
  • Tell the healthcare team about OTC medicines, supplements and allergies.
  • Do not stop long-term treatment abruptly without professional advice unless an emergency clinician instructs otherwise.

🚨 When urgent medical care may be needed

Febrile neutropenia, severe bleeding, acute neurological symptoms, severe treatment toxicity

Emergency symptoms should be assessed urgently rather than managed from this webpage.
