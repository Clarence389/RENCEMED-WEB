Diagnosis / assessment
Anemia Subtype / Etiology Clinical Presentation & History Diagnostic Workup & Laboratory Criteria
Microcytic Anemia (Iron Deficiency Anemia - IDA)
  • General Symptoms: Fatigue, weakness, exertional dyspnea, pallor (conjunctival, palmar).
  • Specific Features: Pica (craving non-nutritive substances), pagophagia (ice craving), angular cheilitis, koilonychia (spoon-shaped nails).
  • History: Chronic blood loss (heavy menses, GI bleeding), poor dietary intake, or malabsorption (celiac disease).
  • CBC: Low Hb/Hct, Low MCV (< 80 fL), Low MCH/MCHC. Elevated RDW.
  • Iron Studies: Serum Ferritin < 30 ng/mL (diagnostic), Low Serum Iron, High TIBC, Low Transferrin Saturation (< 16–20%).
  • Peripheral Blood Smear: Hypochromic, microcytic RBCs with anisopoikilocytosis.
Macrocytic Anemia (Megaloblastic & Non-Megaloblastic)
  • B12 Deficiency: Glossitis (beefy red tongue), neurological symptoms (paresthesias, loss of vibration/proprioception, ataxia, memory loss).
  • Folate Deficiency: Similar oral/GI signs to B12 deficiency but lacks neurological deficits.
  • Non-Megaloblastic Etiologies: Alcohol abuse, hypothyroidism, liver disease, reticulocytosis.
  • CBC: Elevated MCV (> 100 fL), mild leukopenia/thrombocytopenia in severe cases.
  • Vitamin Levels: Serum Vitamin B12 < 200 pg/mL or Serum Folate < 3 ng/mL.
  • Metabolic Markers: Elevated Methylmalonic Acid (MMA) and Homocysteine in B12 deficiency; elevated Homocysteine alone in Folate deficiency.
  • Smear: Hypersegmented neutrophils (≥ 5 lobes) in megaloblastic anemia.
Normocytic Anemia (ACD / CKD / Hemolytic)
  • Anemia of Chronic Disease (ACD): Underlining systemic disease (rheumatoid arthritis, infection, malignancy).
  • Anemia of CKD: Uremic symptoms, progressive reduction in kidney function (eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73m²).
  • Hemolytic Anemia: Jaundice, scleral icterus, dark urine (hemoglobinuria), splenomegaly.
  • CBC: Normal MCV (80–100 fL). Low reticulocyte index in hypoproliferative states; high reticulocytes in hemolysis.
  • Iron Studies in ACD: Low/normal serum iron, Normal/High Serum Ferritin (> 100 ng/mL), Low TIBC.
  • Hemolysis Panel: Elevated Unconjugated Bilirubin, High LDH, Low Haptoglobin, Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT/Coombs) positive in autoimmune hemolysis.
Key monitoring
Monitoring Parameter Target Range & Monitoring Schedule Clinical Purpose & Action Thresholds
Hemoglobin (Hb) & CBC Indices
  • Target Hb: Men: >13.0 g/dL; Non-pregnant women: >12.0 g/dL (CKD target: 10.0–11.5 g/dL).
  • Schedule: Check baseline, then at 4–8 weeks after initiating replacement therapy; every 3 months once stable.
  • Assesses initial hematologic response and adequacy of therapy.
  • Action Threshold: Expect Hb increase of ≥1 g/dL within 2–4 weeks. Failure to rise indicates non-adherence, ongoing bleeding, malabsorption, or incorrect diagnosis.
Reticulocyte Count
  • Schedule: Measure at day 7–10 following initiation of oral/IV iron, B12, or folate replacement.
  • Target: Reticulocytosis peak within 7–14 days.
  • Early marker of functional bone marrow recovery and effective erythropoiesis prior to Hb normalization.
  • Action Threshold: Absence of reticulocyte response at 14 days warrants evaluation for refractory causes or mixed etiologies.
Iron Stores (Ferritin & TSAT)
  • Target Ferritin: >100 ng/mL (>200–500 ng/mL in CKD/heart failure).
  • Target TSAT: 20%–50%.
  • Schedule: Recheck 3 months after starting oral iron, or 4–8 weeks post-IV iron infusion (avoid testing immediately post-IV iron to prevent false elevations).
  • Monitors replenishment of tissue iron reserves and guards against iron overload.
  • Action Threshold: Discontinue iron therapy if Ferritin >500–800 ng/mL or TSAT >50% to prevent hemosiderosis.
Neurological & Safety Surveillance
  • B12 Deficit: Perform focused neurological examination (vibration, proprioception, gait) at each follow-up visit.
  • Gastrointestinal Tolerance: Assess for constipation, dark stools, or nausea at 2–4 weeks.
  • Ensures reversal of neurological impairment (which may become irreversible if untreated long-term).
  • Action Threshold: Severe GI intolerance to oral iron warrants switching to alternate-day dosing or IV iron formulations.
Management principles
Anemia Subtype / Clinical Scenario First-Line Therapeutic Regimens Core Clinical Strategy & Dosing Principles
Iron Deficiency Anemia (IDA)
  • Oral Iron: Ferrous Sulfate 200 mg (65 mg elemental iron) OR Ferrous Fumarate / Gluconate orally once daily or alternate-day dosing.
  • Parenteral (IV) Iron: Ferric Carboxymaltose, Iron Sucrose, or Ferumoxytol if oral route fails, is un-tolerated, or in active IBD/CKD.
  • Alternate-day oral dosing reduces intestinal hepcidin elevation and improves fractional GI absorption with fewer side effects.
  • Identify and address underlying cause of blood loss (e.g., GI workup in men/postmenopausal women).
Vitamin B12 (Cobalamin) Deficiency
  • Parenteral: Cyanocobalamin / Hydroxocobalamin 1000 mcg IM daily for 1 week, then weekly for 4 weeks, then monthly for life (pernicious anemia/malabsorption).
  • High-Dose Oral: Cyanocobalamin 1000–2000 mcg orally daily (effective via passive diffusion if adherent).
  • Always rule out or co-treat folate deficiency; treating folate alone in B12 deficiency can precipitate irreversible neurological degeneration.
Folate Deficiency Anemia
  • Oral Folic Acid: 1–5 mg orally daily for 1–4 months or until complete hematologic recovery.
  • Ensure Vitamin B12 levels are confirmed normal before initiating high-dose folic acid therapy.
  • Provide prophylactic supplementation (400 mcg–5 mg daily) in pregnancy and chronic hemolytic states (e.g., sickle cell disease).
Anemia of Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD)
  • Erythropoiesis-Stimulating Agents (ESAs): Epoetin alfa (50–100 units/kg 3x/week) or Darbepoetin alfa.
  • Iron Supplementation: IV Iron preferred to maintain Ferritin >200 ng/mL and TSAT >20%.
  • Initiate ESAs only when Hb <10 g/dL. Target Hb: 10.0–11.5 g/dL (do not exceed 11.5–12.0 g/dL to avoid cardiovascular/thrombotic events).
  • Ensure iron sufficiency prior to starting ESA therapy to prevent functional iron deficiency.
Follow-up / outcomesCBC and cause-specific markers; Day 4 – 7 Reticulocyte count peaks (Reticulocytosis indicates bone marrow response). Weeks 2 – 4Hemoglobin (Hb) increases by > 1.0–2.0 g/dL. Patient education
Education Domain Key Patient Instructions & Guidance Rationale & Red Flag Warnings
Oral Iron Administration
  • Take iron on an empty stomach with Vitamin C (e.g., orange juice) to enhance absorption.
  • Take every other day if single daily dosing causes upset stomach.
  • Avoid taking with tea, coffee, milk, antacids, or calcium supplements (space by at least 2 hours).
  • Polyphenols, calcium, and antacids significantly impair iron absorption in the gut.
  • Expected Effects: Dark or black stools and mild constipation are normal, non-harmful side effects.
Dietary Recommendations
  • Iron-Rich Foods: Red meat, poultry, fish, beans, lentils, spinach, and fortified cereals.
  • B12 & Folate Sources: Meat, eggs, dairy, fortified plant milks (B12); dark leafy greens, citrus, nuts, and legumes (folate).
  • Dietary changes support recovery but are rarely sufficient on their own to correct severe deficiencies without supplementation.
Medication Adherence
  • Continue iron therapy for 3 to 6 months after blood counts normalize to rebuild body iron stores.
  • Patients with pernicious anemia or total gastrectomy require lifelong Vitamin B12 replacement.
  • Premature discontinuation leads to rapid relapse of anemia once body stores are depleted.
Warning Signs & When to Seek Care
  • Report immediate red flags to healthcare providers: severe chest pain, shortness of breath at rest, dizziness, fainting, or black/tarry stools with fatigue.
  • May indicate acute cardiovascular compromise, severe drop in hemoglobin, or active gastrointestinal bleeding requiring urgent emergency care.
Multidisciplinary roles
Healthcare Discipline Core Responsibilities & Clinical Scope Interprofessional Collaboration Points
Physician / Hematologist
  • Diagnoses underlying anemia etiology (e.g., IDA, B12/folate deficiency, CKD, hemolysis, MDS).
  • Orders diagnostic procedures (e.g., endoscopy for occult GI bleeding, bone marrow aspirate).
  • Prescribes targeted replenishment, ESAs, or immunosuppressive regimens.
  • Refers to gastroenterology or gynecology for active blood loss management.
  • Partners with pharmacists for IV iron dosing protocols and ESA stewardship.
Clinical Pharmacist
  • Evaluates oral iron formulation suitability, dosing frequency (e.g., alternate-day), and absorption interactions.
  • Manages IV iron infusions, screening for hypersensitivity and managing infusion reactions.
  • Monitors ESA therapy targets to prevent over-correction and thromboembolic events.
  • Recommends switching from oral to IV iron in cases of GI intolerance or malabsorption.
  • Coordinates with nursing staff on IV iron safety infusion protocols.
Gastroenterologist / Specialist
  • Performs diagnostic upper/lower endoscopies to identify sources of chronic GI blood loss.
  • Manages inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) or celiac disease driving iron/nutrient malabsorption.
  • Coordinates timing of mucosal healing with hematology/primary care iron restoration goals.
Registered Dietitian
  • Conducts nutritional assessments for dietary iron, B12, and folate deficiencies.
  • Educates patients on heme vs. non-heme iron sources and absorption enhancers/inhibitors.
  • Provides tailored meal planning for strict vegan/vegetarian diets or post-bariatric surgery care.
Individualization
Patient Factor / Domain Clinical Considerations & Cautions Tailored Management Approach
Age & Geriatrics
  • Higher prevalence of unexplained anemia of aging, multifactorial etiologies, and occult GI malignancy.
  • Oral iron causes enhanced GI toxicity and constipation, compounding baseline bowel dysmotility.
  • Utilize low-dose or alternate-day oral iron strategies to improve tolerance.
  • Maintain a lower threshold for initiating IV iron or pursuing GI endoscopic diagnostic workup.
Pregnancy
  • Plasma volume expansion creates physiological dilutional anemia; iron demand escalates dramatically in 2nd/3rd trimesters.
  • Severe anemia is linked to premature delivery, low birth weight, and maternal transfusion risk.
  • Routine iron and folate supplementation recommended during prenatal care.
  • Bypass oral iron in favor of IV iron infusions during the 2nd or 3rd trimester if Hb drops severely or oral iron fails.
Renal & Hepatic Impairment
  • CKD: Erythropoietin deficiency and systemic inflammation drive functional iron deficiency via high hepcidin.
  • Liver Disease: Impaired synthesis of coagulation factors and transport proteins complicatest Diagnostic markers (e.g., elevated baseline Ferritin).
  • Prioritize IV iron over oral routes in advanced CKD (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73m² or HD dependency).
  • Use Transferrin Saturation (TSAT) over Ferritin as the primary operational marker of iron availability in chronic inflammation.
Concurrent Medicines & Allergies
  • Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) and H2 blockers reduce gastric acidity required for optimal non-heme oral iron absorption.
  • History of severe hypersensitivity reactions to previous IV iron formulations.
  • Co-administer oral iron with Vitamin C (Ascorbic Acid) when patients are on acid-suppressive therapy.
  • Select modern low-risk IV formulations (e.g., Ferric Carboxymaltose, Iron Sucrose) and premedicate if allergic history is unclear.
Socioeconomic & Patient Preferences
  • High cost of IV iron formulations and infusion center access limitations.
  • Strict vegan/vegetarian diets or religious preferences regarding blood products.
  • Prescribe low-cost generic oral iron formulations (e.g., Ferrous Sulfate) when financial barriers exist.
  • Provide targeted nutrition counseling for dietary iron/B12 substitutes; respect blood transfusion refusals with aggressive iron/ESA protocols.

Patient Checklist

  • Know the name and purpose of each medicine.
  • Know how and when to take or use treatment.
  • Ask what adverse effects require medical attention.
  • Keep appointments and monitoring tests.
  • Tell the healthcare team about OTC medicines, supplements and allergies.
  • Do not stop long-term treatment abruptly without professional advice unless an emergency clinician instructs otherwise.
