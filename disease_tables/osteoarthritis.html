Clinical framework

What clinicians assess

This page organizes the major clinical decision points for Osteoarthritis. It is designed for patient education and professional revision, not as a substitute for a current guideline, local protocol or individualized clinical assessment.

Diagnosis / assessment
Diagnostic Domain Clinical Features & Diagnostic Criteria Role & Diagnostic Workflow
Clinical Symptom Assessment
  • Age Criteria: Age ≥ 45 years.
  • Activity-Related Joint Pain: Persistent, usage-related pain in targeted joints (knees, hips, hand DIP/PIP, 1st MTP, or spine).
  • Morning Stiffness: Absent or brief morning joint stiffness lasting ≤ 30 minutes.
  • Diagnose clinically without routine imaging in typical presentations (NICE / ACR guidelines).
  • Evaluate functional limitation, gait alterations, and pain impact on daily living activities.
Physical Examination Findings
  • Joint Signatures: Joint line tenderness, coarse crepitus on passive/active movement, joint enlargement (bony hypertrophy).
  • Nodal Features: Heberden's nodes (DIP joints) and Bouchard's nodes (PIP joints).
  • Structural Changes: Restricted range of motion, mild periarticular muscle wasting, or varus/valgus deformity.
  • Differentiate from inflammatory arthropathies (absence of significant warm, soft-tissue synovial swelling or systemic signs).
  • Confirm structural joint involvement and localized mechanical strain.
Imaging (Plain Radiography / MRI)
  • Classic Radiographic Features: Asymmetric joint space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis, osteophyte formation, and subchondral cysts.
  • Indications for Plain X-rays: Diagnostic uncertainty, atypical presentation (younger age < 45, severe rest/night pain), rapid progression, or preoperative surgical planning.
  • MRI: Reserved for suspected soft-tissue pathology (meniscal tears, ligamentous injury, osteonecrosis) or spinal cord compression.
Differential Diagnosis & Red Flags
  • Laboratory Evaluation: Normal inflammatory markers (ESR/CRP) and negative rheumatoid factor (RF/anti-CCP).
  • Synovial Fluid (if joint effusion present): Non-inflammatory clear fluid (WBC < 2000/mm³).
  • Exclude septic arthritis, crystal arthropathies (gout/pseudogout), and rheumatoid arthritis in acute presentations with joint effusion or red flag systemic features (fever, weight loss).
Key monitoring
Monitoring Parameter Clinical Indicators & Assessment Tools Evaluation Frequency & Strategy
Pain Severity & Quality
  • Quantitative Pain Scores: Visual Analog Scale (VAS) or Numerical Rating Scale (NRS 0–10).
  • Pain Characterization: Distinguish weight-bearing mechanical pain from rest/night pain or neuropathic descriptors.
  • Assess at each clinical review (q3–6 months during active intervention).
  • Re-evaluate pain response 2–4 weeks after initiating or altering systemic/topical analgesic therapy.
Functional Capacity & Mobility
  • Validated Functional Indices: WOMAC (Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index) or HOOS/KOOS scores.
  • Physical Markers: Gait stability, walking distance without pain, stair climbing ability, and range of joint motion.
  • Track physical function every 6–12 months to gauge disease progression and response to physical therapy or exercise regimens.
Impact on Daily Living & Quality of Life
  • Functional Performance: Evaluate ability to perform activities of daily living (ADLs) such as dressing, bathing, and transfers.
  • Psychosocial Factors: Assess sleep disruption due to pain, mood changes, and loss of independence.
  • Incorporate brief standardized assessment (e.g., EQ-5D or SF-12) during annual reviews or prior to orthopedic surgical referral.
Analgesic Safety & Surgical Readiness
  • Medication Tolerability: Monitor for NSAID-related GI toxicity, hypertension, renal impairment, or fluid retention.
  • Surgical Indications: Unrelieved severe pain causing major functional impairment despite optimal non-surgical therapy.
  • Check baseline and annual serum creatinine, eGFR, BP, and LFTs if patient uses chronic systemic NSAIDs.
  • Screen for refractory symptoms to trigger timely orthopedic referral for total joint arthroplasty.
Management principles
Management Pillar Core Therapeutic Interventions Implementation Guidelines & Clinical Context
Core Non-Pharmacological Interventions
  • Therapeutic Exercise: Local muscle strengthening, aerobic conditioning, and joint range-of-motion exercises (e.g., swimming, cycling, walking).
  • Weight Management: Target ≥ 5%–10% weight loss in overweight or obese patients with knee or hip OA.
  • First-line for all patients regardless of age or disease severity.
  • Weight reduction directly decreases mechanical load on weight-bearing joints (1 lb loss = 4 lbs reduced knee load per step).
Non-Drug Measures & Orthotics
  • Physical Support: Assistive devices (canes/walking sticks used on contralateral side), knee braces, or orthotic insoles.
  • Local Therapies: Thermotherapy (heat/ice packs) and self-management education.
  • Refer to physiotherapy or occupational therapy for gait assessment and tailored joint-protection strategies.
  • Reduces joint instability and improves safety during daily activities.
First-Line Topical & Oral Analgesia
  • Topical NSAIDs: Topical Diclofenac gel/patch (first-line for knee OA, especially in older adults).
  • Oral NSAIDs / COX-2 Inhibitors: Ibuprofen, Naproxen, or Celecoxib used at the lowest effective dose for the shortest necessary duration.
  • Topical NSAIDs provide equivalent pain relief to oral NSAIDs with significantly lower systemic GI/renal risk.
  • Always co-prescribe a Proton Pump Inhibitor (PPI) when using oral NSAIDs in patients with GI risk.
Step-Up & Intra-Articular Therapies
  • Intra-Articular Corticosteroids: Triamcinolone or Methylprednisolone injections for acute moderate-to-severe painful flares.
  • Centrally-Acting Analgesics: Duloxetine for chronic multi-joint OA or concurrent neuropathic pain features.
  • Intra-articular steroid injections provide short-term relief (typically 1–8 weeks); limit frequency to max 3–4 times per year per joint.
  • Avoid routine systemic opioids due to poor efficacy and high dependence risk.
Follow-up / outcomes
Follow-up Domain Clinical Outcome Targets Longitudinal Safety & Care Strategy
Pain Relief & Functional Recovery
  • Symptom Control: Clinically meaningful reduction in pain score (≥ 30% reduction from baseline).
  • Functional Target: Improved gait, mobility range, and capability to perform ADLs.
  • Re-evaluate at 2–4 weeks after initiating new pharmacological therapies or physical therapy regimens.
  • Inquire about activity levels and reinforce non-pharmacological compliance at every routine visit.
Gastrointestinal (GI) Risk Surveillance
  • Adverse Symptoms: Dyspepsia, epigastric pain, nausea, or signs of GI bleeding (melena, hematemesis).
  • Laboratory Signs: Unexplained drop in hemoglobin/hematocrit.
  • Assess GI tolerability regularly if the patient is on oral systemic NSAIDs.
  • Ensure ongoing co-prescription of a PPI for high-risk patients; switch to topical NSAIDs or non-NSAID options if dyspepsia occurs.
Renal & Cardiovascular Risk Surveillance
  • Renal Indicators: Serum creatinine, eGFR, and fluid retention/peripheral edema.
  • Cardiovascular Indicators: New-onset or worsening hypertension.
  • Check baseline and annual eGFR/BUN and blood pressure in patients taking chronic oral NSAIDs (more frequent in CKD, elderly, or heart failure).
  • Discontinue or step down systemic NSAIDs if eGFR drops below 30 mL/min or if uncontrolled BP occurs.
Medication Deprescribing & Surgical Escalation
  • Analgesic Review: Continued necessity of daily analgesia versus as-needed (PRN) dosing.
  • End-Stage Indications: Persistent severe pain, joint instability, or complete functional disability refractory to conservative therapy.
  • Attempt to taper or discontinue systemic NSAIDs during periods of symptom stability.
  • Refer to orthopedic surgery for total joint replacement evaluation when conservative management fails to maintain acceptable quality of life.
Patient education
Education Domain Core Patient Counseling Messages Verification & Action Plan
Condition Nature & Treatment Goals
  • Joint Health Nature: Reframe OA as an active whole-joint condition that responds positively to movement, rather than simple "wear and tear."
  • Purpose of Treatment: Explain that interventions aim to reduce pain, protect joint function, and preserve mobility rather than provide an instant cure.
  • Reassure patients that physical activity under guidance is safe and strengthens supporting muscles.
  • Set functional goals (e.g., walking without severe discomfort) rather than complete pain elimination.
Lifestyle Measures & Safe Analgesia
  • Exercise & Weight Protection: Encourage low-impact aerobic activity (swimming, cycling, walking) and joint protection strategies.
  • Analgesic Adherence & Safety: Emphasize taking oral NSAIDs at the lowest effective dose with meals/PPIs, and correct application of topical gels.
  • Counsel against unmonitored chronic over-the-counter NSAID consumption.
  • Demonstrate proper application of topical gels directly over affected joint sites.
Urgent Care Warnings & Red Flags
  • Infectious / Acute Red Flags: Advise immediate medical care if experiencing:
    • Hot, red, acutely swollen joint with fever (suspected septic arthritis).
    • Inability to bear weight suddenly or joint locking/giving way.
    • Dark, tarry stools or severe stomach pain (NSAID-related GI bleeding/ulceration).
  • Provide written list of red flag symptoms requiring emergency or urgent clinic review.
  • Highlight specific medication warning signs associated with systemic pain relievers.
Teach-Back Confirmation Method
  • Understanding Check: Confirm the patient grasps their exercise routine, safe pain relief management, and warning signs.
  • Ask the patient to explain in their own words:
    • "What kind of exercise routine will best support your joint health?"
    • "How and when should you safely use your prescribed pain relievers?"
    • "What warning signs mean you should call our office or go to urgent care right away?"
Multidisciplinary roles
Team Discipline Core Responsibilities & Clinical Scope Key Interprofessional Deliverables
Physicians & Orthopedic Specialists
  • Assessment & Diagnosis: Clinical assessment, differential diagnosis, order targeted imaging when diagnosis is uncertain, and perform intra-articular joint injections.
  • Surgical Evaluation: Determine candidacy and timing for total joint arthroplasty in refractory end-stage OA.
  • Establish clinical management plan and oversee medical/surgical escalation pathways.
  • Coordinate referrals to physical therapy, rheumatology, or orthopedic surgery.
Pharmacists
  • Medicine Optimization: Tailor oral/topical analgesic regimens, screen for drug-drug interactions (e.g., NSAIDs with anticoagulants or antihypertensives), and assess GI/renal risks.
  • Safety & Adherence: Recommend gastroprotective PPI co-prescription and support proper topical formulation application.
  • Conduct periodic medication reviews to deprescribe unneeded systemic NSAIDs and manage OTC pain reliever safety.
  • Advise patients on safe dosing thresholds for acetaminophen and topical agents.
Nurses & Allied Health (PT / OT)
  • Surveillance & Mobility Coaching: Track pain scores, joint function, mobility, and medication side effects; design customized exercise and joint-protection regimens.
  • Assistive Tech & Rehabilitation: Fit braces, walking aids, and ergonomics to preserve independence in daily living activities.
  • Deliver structured patient education on joint biomechanics and lifestyle modifications.
  • Coordinate ongoing rehabilitation schedules and monitor functional outcomes.
Patient & Caregivers
  • Active Shared Decision-Making: Collaborate on functional goals and participate in treatment choice (non-pharmacological vs. systemic therapies).
  • Self-Management: Maintain prescribed physical exercise, adhere to agreed medication schedules, and report red-flag symptoms promptly.
  • Consistently perform home exercises and weight protection strategies.
  • Promptly report adverse drug reactions (e.g., epigastric pain) or sudden drops in joint function to the care team.
Individualization
Patient Factor Clinical Impact & Vulnerabilities Tailoring Strategy & Regimen Adjustments
Age Extremes & Frailty
  • Older Adults (> 65 yrs): Heightened susceptibility to NSAID-induced GI bleeding, acute kidney injury, fluid retention, and drug interactions.
  • Fall Risk: Dizziness or sedation from central pain modulators (e.g., duloxetine, opioids) increases fall and fracture risk.
  • Prefer topical NSAIDs as first-line therapy over oral NSAIDs due to superior systemic safety.
  • If oral NSAIDs are necessary, use short courses at lowest doses combined with mandatory PPI co-prescription.
Renal & Hepatic Impairment
  • Renal Impairment (CKD): Oral NSAIDs inhibit renal prostaglandins, causing acute decline in eGFR, sodium retention, and hyperkalemia.
  • Hepatic Impairment: Reduced clearance and increased risk of hepatoxicity with acetaminophen or NSAIDs.
  • CKD (eGFR < 30 mL/min): Avoid systemic NSAIDs completely; rely on topical agents, non-drug physical therapy, or targeted intra-articular steroid injections.
  • Cap acetaminophen dosage to ≤ 2 g/day in underlying hepatic dysfunction or chronic alcohol exposure.
Cardiovascular & GI Comorbidities
  • High CV Risk: Systemic NSAIDs (especially COX-2 selective agents) increase thrombotic events, heart failure exacerbations, and hypertension.
  • Peptic Ulcer Disease / GI Bleed History: Non-selective NSAIDs without gastroprotection pose high ulceration risks.
  • In high CV risk, prefer Naproxen if systemic NSAIDs are unavoidable, or utilize non-pharmacological modalities.
  • In high GI risk, select COX-2 selective inhibitors (e.g., Celecoxib) combined with PPI, or substitute with topical preparations.
Socioeconomic Barriers & Preferences
  • Access Limitations: Cost barriers to physical therapy, joint braces, or newer SNRI medications.
  • Lifestyle Considerations: Patient preferences regarding joint replacement surgery versus long-term conservative care.
  • Prescribe accessible, structured home-based exercise routines when formal PT access is constrained.
  • Align therapeutic escalation (e.g., intra-articular therapies or joint replacement referral) with patient preference and tolerance for invasive interventions.

Patient Checklist

  • Know the name and purpose of each medicine.
  • Know how and when to take or use treatment.
  • Ask what adverse effects require medical attention.
  • Keep appointments and monitoring tests.
  • Tell the healthcare team about OTC medicines, supplements and allergies.
  • Do not stop long-term treatment abruptly without professional advice unless an emergency clinician instructs otherwise.
