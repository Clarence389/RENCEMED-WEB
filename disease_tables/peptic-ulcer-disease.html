What clinicians assess

This page organizes the major clinical decision points for Peptic Ulcer Disease. It is designed for patient education and professional revision, not as a substitute for a current guideline, local protocol or individualized clinical assessment.

Diagnosis / assessment
Diagnostic Domain Clinical Assessment & Presentation Diagnostic Role & Clinical Workflow
Clinical History & Symptom Profile
  • Epigastric Pain: Burning, aching, or gnawing epigastric discomfort.
  • Duodenal Ulcer Pattern: Pain occurs 1.5–3 hours post-meal, often relieved by food or antacids; classic night pain waking patient.
  • Gastric Ulcer Pattern: Pain precipitated or worsened by food intake; early satiety, bloating, or nausea.
  • Etiology History: Chronic NSAID/aspirin use, heavy alcohol intake, smoking, or history of H. pylori.
  • Guides initial diagnostic index of suspicion.
  • Differentiates non-ulcer dyspepsia from peptic ulceration, though definitive distinction requires endosocopy or non-invasive testing.
Alarm Features (Red Flags)
  • Gastrointestinal Bleeding: Hematemesis, "coffee-ground" emesis, melena, or unexplained iron-deficiency anemia.
  • Malignancy/Obstruction Indicators: Progressive dysphagia, odynophagia, recurrent vomiting, persistent anorexia, or unexplained weight loss.
  • Age Cut-off: New-onset dyspepsia in patients ≥ 55 years (or regional threshold ≥ 60 years).
  • Urgent Endoscopy Referral: Presence of ANY alarm feature mandates prompt Upper Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (EGD) to rule out gastric malignancy or strictures.
  • Avoid empirical acid-suppression therapy prior to EGD in patients with red flags.
H. pylori Non-Invasive Testing
  • Urea Breath Test (UBT): High sensitivity and specificity for active infection.
  • Stool Antigen Test (SAT): Reliable non-invasive alternative for active infection.
  • Serology (IgG): Indicates exposure; cannot distinguish past from active infection (not recommended for routine confirmation).
  • Indicated for uninvestigated dyspepsia in patients < 55 years without alarm features ("test-and-treat" strategy).
  • Crucial: Hold PPIs for ≥ 2 weeks and antibiotics/bismuth for ≥ 4 weeks before UBT/SAT to prevent false negatives.
Upper GI Endoscopy (EGD) & Biopsy
  • Visual Direct Assessment: Identifies exact mucosal defect size, depth, location, and stigmata of recent hemorrhage (Forrest classification).
  • Mucosal Biopsies: Rapid Urease Test (RUT / CLOtest), histology, or biopsy of gastric ulcer margins to exclude carcinoma.
  • Gold-standard diagnostic modality for peptic ulcer disease.
  • Required for alarm symptoms, refractory ulcers, or high-risk NSAID-induced lesions.
Key monitoring
Monitoring Parameter Clinical Indicators & Target Thresholds Evaluation Strategy & Clinical Action
Symptom Resolution & Response
  • Epigastric Relief: Marked reduction or complete resolution of burning epigastric pain, postprandial fullness, and nocturnal pain.
  • Treatment Failure: Persistent or worsening dyspeptic symptoms after 2–4 weeks of acid suppression or eradication therapy.
  • Evaluate symptom trajectory at 2–4 weeks post-initiation.
  • Lack of symptom response warrants assessment for non-adherence, H. pylori resistance, persistent NSAID use, or repeat endoscopy.
Gastrointestinal Bleeding Surveillance
  • Overt Bleeding Signs: Absence of melena (black tarry stools), hematemesis, or "coffee-ground" emesis.
  • Hemodynamic & Lab Parameters: Stable blood pressure, pulse, and hemoglobin/hematocrit levels without acute drop.
  • Monitor vital signs and serial hemoglobin in acute ulcer bleeding settings until stabilized.
  • Re-evaluate stool color and screen for orthostatic hypotension during outpatient follow-up visits.
H. pylori Eradication Testing
  • Eradication Target: Negative Urea Breath Test (UBT) or Stool Antigen Test (SAT) confirming complete bacterial clearance.
  • Perform non-invasive test (UBT/SAT) at least 4 weeks post-completion of antibiotic therapy and 2 weeks post-PPI cessation.
  • Confirmation is essential for all patients with documented H. pylori ulceration.
Endoscopic Healing Surveillance
  • Mucosal Healing: Complete re-epithelialization of gastric or duodenal ulcer bed on repeat endoscopy.
  • Gastric Ulcers: Repeat EGD at 8–12 weeks is recommended to confirm healing and exclude gastric malignancy via biopsy.
  • Duodenal Ulcers: Repeat EGD is generally unnecessary unless symptoms persist or rebleed occurs.
Management principles
Therapeutic Pillar Standard Pharmacotherapy & Regimens Clinical Strategy & Duration
H. pylori Eradication Therapy
  • Bismuth Quadruple Therapy (First-line): PPI (standard dose BID) + Bismuth subsalicylate + Metronidazole + Tetracycline for 14 days.
  • Clarithromycin Triple Therapy: PPI BID + Amoxicillin + Clarithromycin for 14 days (only if local macrolide resistance < 15%).
  • Mandatory 14-day duration to maximize eradication rates and mitigate resistance.
  • Follow eradication with 2–4 additional weeks of standard PPI therapy to ensure complete mucosal healing.
NSAID / Aspirin-Induced Ulcer Strategy
  • Discontinuation: Stop offender NSAID/aspirin immediately if clinically feasible.
  • Acid Suppression: Standard or double-dose PPI (e.g., Omeprazole 20–40 mg daily) or Potassium-Competitive Acid Blocker (P-CAB, e.g., Vonoprazan).
  • Treat with PPI for 4–8 weeks (4 weeks for duodenal, 8 weeks for gastric).
  • If NSAID/aspirin cannot be discontinued, co-prescribe a daily PPI or switch to a COX-2 selective inhibitor to prevent recurrence.
Mucosal Protection & Cytoprotection
  • Sucralfate: Forms a protective barrier over the ulcer bed (1g QID taken on an empty stomach).
  • Misoprostol: Synthetic prostaglandin E1 analog for NSAID-induced ulcer prevention (contraindicated in pregnancy).
  • Used primarily as adjuncts or alternatives when PPIs are poorly tolerated or contraindicated.
  • Sucralfate requires separate dosing timing from other oral drugs due to absorption binding.
Acute Bleeding & Refractory Ulcer Management
  • IV Acid Suppression: High-dose IV PPI bolus followed by continuous infusion or twice-daily IV PPI dosing.
  • Endoscopic Homeostasis: Thermal, mechanical (clips), or injection therapy during early EGD (< 24 hours).
  • Resuscitate with fluids/transfusion as needed before endoscopic therapy.
  • Transition to oral PPI BID once oral intake is restored.
Follow-up / outcomes
Follow-up Domain Clinical Outcome Indicators Longitudinal Safety & Surveillance Strategy
Symptom Response & Healing Evaluation
  • Symptom Trajectory: Resolution of epigastric pain, postprandial nausea, and early satiety within 2–4 weeks.
  • Refractory Symptoms: Persistent epigastric pain despite completing full course of acid suppression therapy.
  • Schedule clinical follow-up at 4–8 weeks to confirm complete symptomatic relief.
  • If symptoms persist, assess for non-adherence, persistent H. pylori, continued NSAID/aspirin use, or refer for EGD/biopsy.
Treatment Adherence & Regimen Completion
  • Eradication Regimen Adherence: Complete intake of complex multi-drug quadruple or triple therapy regimens.
  • Pill Burden Barriers: High dose frequency (e.g., QID bismuth/tetracycline) leading to premature discontinuation.
  • Emphasize that completing the full 14-day H. pylori regimen is critical to prevent treatment failure and bacterial resistance.
  • Inquire specifically about pill-taking timing in relation to meals and acid suppression.
Eradication Confirmation & Re-Testing
  • Test Selection: Urea Breath Test (UBT) or Stool Antigen Test (SAT) used as post-treatment confirmation.
  • Washout Period Compliance: Adequate off-drug window before re-testing.
  • Perform UBT or SAT at least 4 weeks post-antibiotics and 2 weeks post-PPI cessation.
  • If eradication fails, initiate second-line rescue regimen (e.g., Bismuth Quadruple or Levofloxacin Triple Therapy).
Adverse Effect & Long-Term Safety Checks
  • Antibiotic/Bismuth Tolerability: Diarrhea, dysgeusia (metallic taste), dark stools/tongue (bismuth), or GI upset.
  • Chronic PPI Effects: Vitamin B12 deficiency, hypomagnesemia, elevated risk of C. difficile, or rebound acid hypersecretion upon stopping.
  • Counsel that dark stool from bismuth is benign and temporary.
  • Step down or discontinue long-term PPI therapy once ulcer healing and H. pylori eradication are achieved, unless long-term gastroprotection is indicated.
Patient education
Education Domain Core Patient Counseling Messages Verification & Action Plan
Diagnosis & Purpose of Treatment
  • Ulcer Concept: Explain that a peptic ulcer is a sore in the lining of the stomach or upper small intestine, commonly caused by H. pylori bacteria or pain relievers (NSAIDs).
  • Treatment Purpose: Clarify that acid reducers allow the mucosal lining to heal, while antibiotics eliminate bacterial infection to prevent ulcer recurrence.
  • Reassure the patient that ulcers can fully heal with complete regimen adherence.
  • Set expectations regarding the timeline for symptom relief versus total mucosal healing.
Medication Technique & Strict Adherence
  • PPI Timing: Take proton pump inhibitors 30–60 minutes before the first meal of the day for maximum acid suppression efficacy.
  • Antibiotic Regimen Completion: Stress taking all prescribed antibiotics for the full 14 days, even if pain resolves early, to avoid treatment failure and resistance.
  • Provide a simple daily dosing schedule card for complex 3-drug or 4-drug eradication regimens.
  • Inform patients that bismuth salts may temporarily turn stool or tongue black (benign effect).
Lifestyle Modifications & Trigger Avoidance
  • NSAID Caution: Advise avoiding over-the-counter NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen, naproxen) and aspirin unless explicitly guided by a doctor.
  • Dietary & Habits: Encourage smoking cessation, limiting alcohol intake, and avoiding late-night meals or personal dietary triggers that exacerbate pain.
  • Recommend safer pain management alternatives (e.g., acetaminophen) when clinically appropriate.
  • Provide referral resources for tobacco and alcohol cessation support.
Urgent Red Flags & Teach-Back Confirmation
  • Seek Immediate Care For:
    • Black, tarry stools (melena) or vomiting blood / "coffee-ground" material.
    • Sudden, severe, sharp abdominal pain (suspected perforation).
    • Unexplained dizziness, fainting, or severe weakness.
  • Teach-Back Confirmation: Ask the patient to explain in their own words:
    • "Why is it important to complete all 14 days of your antibiotic pills?"
    • "When should you take your morning acid suppression medication?"
    • "What warning signs mean you need emergency medical attention?"
Multidisciplinary roles
Team Discipline Core Responsibilities & Clinical Scope Key Interprofessional Deliverables
Physicians & Gastroenterologists
  • Assessment & Diagnosis: Conduct comprehensive risk evaluations, evaluate alarm features, perform EGD endoscopy, and establish etiology (H. pylori vs. NSAID-induced).
  • Prescribing Strategy: Prescribe evidence-based eradication or acid-suppression regimens and manage acute bleeding complications.
  • Establish clear treatment plan and determine necessity for follow-up endoscopy.
  • Coordinate surgical or interventional radiology consultation for refractory bleeding or perforation.
Pharmacists
  • Medication Optimization: Review multi-drug eradication therapies for appropriate dosing, local resistance considerations, and drug-drug interactions (e.g., PPIs with Clopidogrel).
  • Adherence & Tolerability: Counsel on proper drug-to-meal timing, manage side effects (e.g., dysgeusia, nausea), and promote 14-day completion.
  • Perform medication reconciliation to eliminate unnecessary OTC NSAIDs or antiplatelet overlaps.
  • Provide structured dosing schedule guides to simplify complex eradication combinations.
Nurses & Clinical Staff
  • Surveillance & Monitoring: Monitor vital signs, assess for signs of GI bleeding (melena, hematemesis), and coordinate follow-up UBT/SAT testing.
  • Patient Education: Reiterate red flag warnings, reinforce administration rules, and assess treatment adherence.
  • Deliver standardized patient education on alarm symptoms and urgent care triggers.
  • Schedule non-invasive H. pylori eradication confirmation testing at the 4-week post-treatment window.
Patient & Caregivers
  • Active Shared Participation: Participate in shared decision-making regarding treatment plans and commit to completing full antibiotic courses.
  • Self-Management & Vigilance: Avoid ulcerogenic triggers (NSAIDs, smoking, excessive alcohol) and report bleeding or severe pain immediately.
  • Consistently adhere to daily dosing instructions for acid suppression and eradication therapies.
  • Promptly report black stools, persistent vomiting, or worsening epigastric pain to the clinical team.
Individualization
Patient Factor Clinical Impact & Vulnerabilities Tailoring Strategy & Regimen Adjustments
Penicillin Allergy
  • Amoxicillin Contraindication: Standard first-line regimens containing amoxicillin cannot be used in confirmed IgE-mediated penicillin allergy.
  • Utilize Bismuth Quadruple Therapy (PPI + Bismuth + Metronidazole + Tetracycline) as preferred first-line.
  • If bismuth is unavailable, substitute Metronidazole for Amoxicillin in triple therapy (only if local metronidazole resistance is low).
Renal or Hepatic Impairment
  • Renal Clearance Limits: H2RA doses require significant adjustment in renal impairment to prevent CNS toxicity; tetracycline should be avoided in severe CKD.
  • Hepatic Metabolism: Severe hepatic impairment decreases clearance of PPIs and metronidazole.
  • Adjust H2RA doses or select PPIs (which do not require renal dose modification).
  • Reduce metronidazole dosage in severe hepatic dysfunction; monitor liver enzymes if using high-dose PPI regimens.
Pregnancy & Lactation
  • Teratogenic Risks: Misoprostol is strictly contraindicated (causes uterine contractions/abortion); tetracycline causes fetal bone/tooth malformations.
  • Utilize antacids or H2RAs (e.g., Famotidine) as initial choices; PPIs (e.g., Omeprazole) are used for refractory cases.
  • Defer routine H. pylori eradication until after delivery and breastfeeding unless severe bleeding/ulceration is present.
Concurrent Cardiovascular / Antiplatelet Meds
  • Bleeding Risk & Drug Interaction: High GI rebleed risk with dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) or anticoagulants; potential CYP2C19 interaction between Omeprazole and Clopidogrel.
  • Co-prescribe a prophylactic daily PPI for all high-risk patients requiring long-term antiplatelet/anticoagulant therapy.
  • Select Pantoprazole or Rabeprazole instead of Omeprazole to minimize CYP2C19 drug interactions with Clopidogrel.

Patient Checklist

  • Know the name and purpose of each medicine.
  • Know how and when to take or use treatment.
  • Ask what adverse effects require medical attention.
  • Keep appointments and monitoring tests.
  • Tell the healthcare team about OTC medicines, supplements and allergies.
  • Do not stop long-term treatment abruptly without professional advice unless an emergency clinician instructs otherwise.
