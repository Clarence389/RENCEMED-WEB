What clinicians assess

This page organizes the major clinical decision points for Epilepsy. It is designed for patient education and professional revision, not as a substitute for a current guideline, local protocol or individualized clinical assessment.

Diagnosis / assessment
Assessment Domain Clinical History & Seizure Semiology EEG & Neuroimaging Protocols
Tonic & Clonic Semiology
(Convulsive Features)
  • Tonic Phase: Sudden, sustained muscle stiffening lasting seconds, frequently accompanied by an expiratory groan and upward eye deviation.
  • Clonic Phase: Rhythmic, synchronous, bilateral muscle jerking following the tonic period.
  • History Context: Document post-ictal confusion, tongue biting (typically lateral), urinary incontinence, and total event duration.
  • EEG Findings: Typically demonstrates generalized paroxysmal fast activity or spike-wave discharges; ictal patterns show rhythmic spike progression.
  • Imaging Indication: High-resolution structural **Brain MRI** is indicated to rule out focal structural abnormalities (e.g., cortical dysplasia, tumors, scars) if focal onset is suspected.
Myoclonic Semiology
  • Clinical Presentation: Sudden, brief, shock-like involuntary single or repetitive muscle contractions without noticeable consciousness impairment.
  • Anamnesis Triggers: Frequently cluster in the morning shortly after awakening. Ask about accidental drops of objects (e.g., breakfast cups or toothbrushes).
  • EEG Findings: Characterized by rapid, generalized **polyspike-and-wave** complexes, often synchronous with the physical muscle twitch.
  • Imaging Indication: Often normal in primary generalized syndromes (e.g., Juvenile Myoclonic Epilepsy), but imaging remains essential if onset is asymmetric or progressive cognitive deterioration occurs.
Absence Semiology
(Non-Convulsive Features)
  • Clinical Presentation: Sudden, brief behavioral arrest or staring spells lasting 5–15 seconds with abrupt onset and termination. No post-ictal confusion.
  • Minor Automations: May feature subtle eyelid fluttering, lip-smacking, or small hand twitches during the detachment window.
  • EEG Findings: Gold standard diagnosis showing highly classic, symmetric, generalized **3 Hz spike-and-wave discharges**.
  • Provocation Testing: Hyperventilation for 3–5 minutes in-office frequently triggers both the electrographic and clinical event.
  • Imaging Indication: Generally unindicated in classic childhood absence unless atypical features appear.
Comprehensive Baseline History
  • Collect witness or video accounts detailing clear pre-ictal auras (e.g., rising epigastric sensation, olfactory shifts), micro-behaviors at onset, and focal vs. generalized components.
  • Review family epilepsy lineage, developmental milestones, febrile seizure histories, and traumatic brain injury exposure.
  • **Standard Baseline Order:** Secure a standard wake/sleep EEG. Consider long-term video-EEG monitoring if events remain undocumented or mimic psychogenic non-epileptic seizures (PNES).
  • Utilize emergency Non-Contrast **Brain CT** only to rule out acute intracranial hemorrhage or stroke following a first new-onset convulsion.
Key monitoring
Monitoring Domain Clinical Indicators & Surveillance Targets Evaluation Protocol & Frequency
Seizure Frequency
  • **Symptom Resolution:** Quantify changes in breakthrough seizure patterns, semiology shifts, clusters, or prolonged episodes.
  • Identify distinct trigger patterns (such as sleep deprivation, missed doses, stress, or menses).
  • Track any occurrence of status epilepticus episodes.
  • Evaluate patient-maintained **seizure logs** or specialized digital tracking applications at every clinical outpatient checkup.
  • Mandate interview verification with primary caregivers or family eye-witnesses to capture subtle absence or focal events.
Medication Adherence
  • Confirm rigorous compliance to daily Anti-Seizure Medication (ASM) intervals.
  • Identify systemic compliance blockades including cognitive impairments, financial strain, complex multi-dose schedules, or fear of toxic side effects.
  • Utilize open, non-punitive screening conversations (e.g., *"With busy schedules, it can be remarkably difficult to take pills perfectly. How many doses have been missed this month?"*).
  • Verify pharmacy refill tracking patterns and perform compliance audits prior to attempting dose modifications.
Adverse Effects
  • **Acute/Dose-Related:** Screen for dizziness, ataxia, diplopia (double vision), sedation, or cognitive slowing.
  • **Idiosyncratic Safety:** Watch for early dangerous signs such as severe drug rashes (hypersensitivity) or acute behavioral changes.
  • **Chronic Impact:** Monitor for long-term weight variance, bone density loss, or gingival hyperplasia.
  • Routinely apply targeted symptom screening protocols at the initiation of therapy and following any dosage titration.
  • Incorporate specialized behavioral screening questionnaires to evaluate mood impacts, particularly depression or suicidality risks associated with specific ASMs.
Relevant Laboratories
  • **Therapeutic Drug Monitoring (TDM):** Track total/free blood serum levels for specific legacy agents (e.g., phenytoin, valproate, carbamazepine) to ensure therapeutic windows.
  • **Organ Safety Monitoring:** Monitor for profound ASM complications like hyponatremia, bone marrow suppression, or drug-induced hepatotoxicity.
  • **Testing Protocol:**
    • Order a **Complete Blood Count (CBC) and Liver Function Tests (LFTs)** at baseline, 1 month, 3 months, and then annually for valproate or carbamazepine.
    • Obtain **Serum Sodium** checks for oxcarbazepine/carbamazepine.
    • Check TDM levels selectively if toxicity is suspected, breakthrough seizures occur, or a change in pregnancy status arises.
Management principles
Selection Axis First-Line Preferred ASMs Clinical Caveats, Modifiers & Contraindications
Seizure Type Selection
  • Focal Onset: Lamotrigine, Levetiracetam, Lacosamide, Oxcarbazepine, or Carbamazepine.
  • Generalized (Tonic-Clonic): Valproate, Lamotrigine, Levetiracetam, or Topiramate.
  • Absence Seizures: Ethosuximide or Valproate.
  • Myoclonic Seizures: Valproate, Levetiracetam, or Topiramate.
  • Critical Worsening Risks: Narrow-spectrum sodium channel blockers (e.g., carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, phenytoin) **must be avoided** in generalized absence or myoclonic epilepsies, as they can paradoxically exacerbate seizure severity and frequency.
Patient Profiles & Comorbidities
  • Geriatric Populations: Lamotrigine, Levetiracetam, or Lacosamide (initiated at lower baseline starting doses with slow titration).
  • Comorbid Depression/Anxiety: Lamotrigine or Valproate (mood-stabilizing benefits).
  • Comorbid Migraine: Topiramate or Valproate.
  • Geriatric Risks: Avoid older enzyme-inducing agents to safeguard bone mineral density and lower fall risks. Watch for oxcarbazepine/carbamazepine-induced hyponatremia, especially alongside concurrent diuretics.
  • Psychiatric Risks: Exercise caution with levetiracetam in active mood or behavioral conditions due to a risk of paradoxical irritability or agitation.
Childbearing Potential & Pregnancy
  • Preferred Framework: Levetiracetam, Lamotrigine, or Oxcarbazepine monotherapy.
  • Enforce mandatory preconception co-prescription of high-dose **Folic Acid (≥ 0.4–5 mg daily)**.
  • Teratogenic Warning: **Valproate is strictly contraindicated** due to high risks of major congenital malformations (neural tube defects) and poor long-term neurodevelopmental/IQ outcomes.
  • Avoid topiramate due to risks of cleft palate and infants born small for gestational age.
  • Monitor serum ASM levels closely during pregnancy, as clearance rates drastically accelerate, requiring proactive dose escalations.
Metabolic & Drug Interactions
  • Preferred Polypharmacy Safe Agents: Levetiracetam, Lacosamide, Gabapentin, or Pregabalin exhibit minimal to zero cytochrome P450 enzyme interactions.
  • Enzyme Inducers: Older legacy agents (carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital) strongly induce hepatic CYP450 systems, severely neutralizing oral contraceptives, anticoagulants (DOACs/warfarin), statins, and chemotherapies.
  • Enzyme Inhibitors: Valproate acts as a liver metabolism inhibitor, doubling serum levels of lamotrigine and drastically increasing the hazard of life-threatening Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS) rash. Cut lamotrigine titration rates by 50% if used concurrently.
Follow-up / outcomes
Follow-up Focus Desired Clinical Outcomes Protocol & Actionable Steps
Seizure Control
  • **Complete Freedom:** Achieve 100% resolution of breakthrough clinical seizures.
  • Minimization of injury risks, status epilepticus occurrences, or functional disruptions.
  • Review and analyze patient-maintained **seizure calendars** or digital tracking summaries at every clinic visit.
  • Re-evaluate diagnosis, look for structural changes, or review medication choices if breakthrough seizures occur despite reaching target medication thresholds.
Adverse Effects
  • **Tolerability Stabilization:** Manage and balance dose-dependent issues (such as dizziness, ataxia, double vision, or daytime drowsiness).
  • Maintain clear baseline bone and cognitive health metrics.
  • Screen for behavioral or mood shifts (such as emerging depression or irritability) at every dose increase.
  • Review laboratory panels (such as Complete Blood Counts, Liver Function Tests, or Serum Sodium) based on the specific drug profile.
Treatment Adherence
  • **Consistent Compliance:** Maintain strict adherence to daily dosing schedules without omitted intervals.
  • Confirm clear patient or caregiver understanding of life safety guidelines (such as water safety and driving laws).
  • Assess compliance using non-judgmental tracking discussions before making any dosage changes.
  • Emphasize that abruptly stopping antiseizure medications poses an extreme safety hazard, with the potential to trigger life-threatening status epilepticus.
Drug Levels
(When Indicated)
  • **Therapeutic Maintenance:** Keep medication levels within the optimal therapeutic window during complex clinical shifts.
  • Identify unexpected metabolic changes early.
  • **Targeted Level Checks:** Avoid routine checking for modern, predictable agents. Order serum drug levels selectively to:
    • Investigate clinical signs of toxicity or sudden treatment failure.
    • Establish an individual baseline level after reaching maintenance targets.
    • Monitor safety during pregnancy, when rapid clearance rates may require frequent dosage adjustments.
Patient education
Education Element Patient Counseling Framework Teach-Back Verification Question
The Diagnosis Explain that the brain coordinates everything through tiny electrical signals. Epilepsy means that sometimes, a sudden, temporary "electrical storm" or surge occurs in the brain's wiring. This surge briefly disrupts the brain's normal messages, resulting in a temporary change in movement, awareness, or behavior known as a seizure.
"To make sure I explained this clearly, how would you describe what is happening in the brain during a seizure to a friend?"
Purpose of Treatment Clarify that daily antiseizure medicines are not a fast cure to repair old brain tissue, but act like a stabilizer or continuous dampening shield. Their job is to calm down the brain's hypersensitive cells and prevent electrical surges from triggering breakthrough events, keeping the patient safe.
"What is the main goal we are aiming for by having you take this protective shield medicine every single day?"
Medication Adherence Emphasize that the medicine must be taken exactly on time every day to keep a steady, protective level in the blood. Warn the patient that missing doses lowers this shield and can cause severe, rebound seizures. **Never stop this medication abruptly**, as doing so can trigger continuous, life-threatening seizures.
"If you accidentally miss a dose, or if you feel completely seizure-free for months, why is it vital not to stop taking your pills?"
Lifestyle Measures Focus on high-priority life safety: Maintain a highly consistent sleep routine, as sleep deprivation is a powerful seizure trigger. Avoid alcohol and recreational drugs completely. Enforce strict **water safety rules** (take showers instead of baths, never swim unsupervised). Review state driving laws, which require a mandatory seizure-free period before operating a vehicle.
"What are two important changes you need to make at home regarding your sleep and bathroom safety to lower your seizure risks?"
Monitoring & First Aid Teach the patient or family to maintain a detailed seizure diary (tracking date, time, length, and description). Train caregivers in **Seizure First Aid**: Stay calm, time the event, cushion the head, clear the area, gently turn them onto their side to keep the airway open, and **never put anything inside their mouth**.
"If a family member sees you having a convulsive seizure at home, what are the exact steps they should take to keep you safe?"
Urgent Care Red Flags Instruct caregivers to call emergency services immediately or administer prescribed rescue medication (like nasal midazolam) if:
  • An individual seizure lasts **longer than 5 minutes**.
  • A second seizure starts immediately after the first without the person fully waking up in between.
  • The seizure occurs in water, a serious physical injury occurs, or breathing remains labored post-event.
"What is the exact time limit for a single seizure before your caregiver must call emergency services immediately?"
Multidisciplinary rolesDoctors assess and diagnose; pharmacists optimize medicines, identify interactions and support adherence; nurses monitor, educate and coordinate care; patients participate in decisions, take medicines as agreed and report problems promptly.
Individualization
Individualization Vector Clinical ASM Customization & Selection Safety Protocols & Specific Exclusions
Age & Pregnancy Potential
  • Geriatric Profiles: Prioritize lamotrigine, levetiracetam, or lacosamide due to stable pharmacokinetics. Adopt a "start low and go slow" titration protocol to minimize central nervous system toxicities (ataxia, confusion).
  • Pediatrics: Adapt formulation to liquids or dispersible tablets. Select broad-spectrum options that minimize disruption to cognitive development.
  • Childbearing Potential & Pregnancy: Proactively prescribe high-dose folic acid (≥ 4–5 mg/day). Optimize preferred monotherapies (levetiracetam, lamotrigine) before conception.
  • **Absolute Contraindication:** Valproate is prohibited due to high teratogenic malformation risks and severe neurodevelopmental decline. Avoid topiramate due to cleft lip/palate risks.
Renal, Hepatic & Comorbid Burden
  • Renal Clearance (eGFR): Levetiracetam, gabapentin, and pregabalin are cleared exclusively by the kidneys. Down-titrate maintenance doses step-by-step as eGFR falls.
  • Hepatic Insufficiency: Prioritize renal-cleared options. Heavily reduce doses or strictly avoid highly hepatotoxic agents like valproate or phenytoin.
  • Psychiatric Comorbidities: In patients with severe depression, anxiety, or borderline features, avoid levetiracetam due to risks of behavioral agitation ("levetiracetam rage"). Prefer lamotrigine or valproate for dual mood-stabilizing effects.
  • Bone Health: Avoid older enzyme-inducers in patients with osteoporosis to prevent acceleration of bone mineral loss.
Allergies & Polypharmacy
  • Genetic Screening (HLA-B*1502): Mandatory for patients of Asian ancestry prior to initiating carbamazepine or oxcarbazepine to identify risks for Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS).
  • Cross-Reactivity Guarding: If an aromatic ASM causes a rash, avoid other aromatics (phenytoin, phenobarbital) due to high cross-sensitivity rates.
  • Enzyme Interference Risks: Avoid older enzyme-inducing ASMs (carbamazepine, phenytoin) in patients taking oral contraceptives (renders them ineffective), direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), or chemotherapies.
  • Valproate + Lamotrigine Mixture: Valproate doubles lamotrigine serum levels. If used together, cut the lamotrigine titration schedule by 50% to prevent SJS.
Socioeconomic Factors & Preferences
  • Financial Accessibility: Prioritize affordable, older generation first-line generics (e.g., standard generic levetiracetam or carbamazepine) if modern proprietary formulations are trapped behind high tier-copays.
  • Lab Access Limits: Avoid agents requiring routine drug monitoring (phenytoin, lithium) if the patient lacks consistent medical transport or lab insurance.
  • Patient Preference Alignment: Incorporate personal aesthetic or lifestyle choices into drug profiles (e.g., avoiding valproate if cosmetic concerns like severe alopecia or rapid weight gain are dealbreakers).
  • Tailor dosing frequencies (once-daily vs. twice-daily extended release) to match active employment or school schedules to secure natural compliance.

Patient Checklist

  • Know the name and purpose of each medicine.
  • Know how and when to take or use treatment.
  • Ask what adverse effects require medical attention.
  • Keep appointments and monitoring tests.
  • Tell the healthcare team about OTC medicines, supplements and allergies.
  • Do not stop long-term treatment abruptly without professional advice unless an emergency clinician instructs otherwise.

🚨 When urgent medical care may be needed

Prolonged seizure, repeated seizures without recovery, injury or breathing difficulty
