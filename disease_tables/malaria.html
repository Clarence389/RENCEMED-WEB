Diagnosis / assessment
Assessment Domain Clinical Indicators & Diagnostic Criteria Testing Protocol & Appropriateness
Clinical Suspicion & Exposure
  • Symptom Profile: Acute febrile illness (fever ≥ 37.5°C or history of fever), chills, rigors, headache, myalgia, arthralgia, and fatigue.
  • Epidemiological Context: Residence in or recent travel (within past 1–4 weeks) to an endemic malaria zone; exposure without chemoprophylaxis.
  • Maintain a high index of suspicion in non-immune travelers and pregnant women presenting with fever.
  • Assess for acute illness onset and differential considerations (e.g., Dengue, Typhoid, Leptospirosis, Bacterial Sepsis).
Parasitological Confirmation
  • Light Microscopy (Gold Standard): Giemsa-stained thick and thin blood smears.
    • Thick Smear: High sensitivity for parasitemia detection and density quantification.
    • Thin Smear: Species identification (P. falciparum, P. vivax, P. ovale, P. malariae, P. knowlesi) and stippling morphology.
  • Rapid Diagnostic Tests (RDTs): Detect parasite antigens (HRP2 for P. falciparum; pLDH or Aldolase for pan-species).
  • Perform parasitological testing **prior to initiating antimalarial treatment** in all suspected cases where feasible.
  • If initial microscopy is negative but suspicion remains high, repeat blood smears every 12–24 hours for up to 72 hours.
  • Use RDTs when quality-assured microscopy is unavailable or delayed.
Severity Assessment & Red Flags
  • Uncomplicated Malaria: Positive parasitemia without clinical or laboratory evidence of vital organ dysfunction.
  • Severe Malaria Criteria (WHO): Presence of P. falciparum (or P. vivax/knowlesi) asexual forms plus ≥ 1 red flag marker:
    • Impaired consciousness (Glasgow Coma Scale < 11 or Blantyre < 3).
    • Prostration / extreme weakness (inability to sit or walk without assistance).
    • Multiple convulsions (> 2 in 24 hours).
    • Respiratory distress (acidotic breathing, pulmonary edema).
    • Severe anemia (Hb < 7 g/dL or Hct < 20% with parasitemia > 100,000/µL).
    • Jaundice with evidence of other organ dysfunction.
  • Immediately evaluate baseline vitals, oxygen saturation, capillary refill, and mental state upon presentation.
  • Order immediate STAT lab workup for suspected severe malaria:
    • Blood glucose (rule out severe hypoglycemia < 40 mg/dL).
    • Venous Blood Gas / Lactate (assess metabolic acidosis).
    • Complete Blood Count (CBC) & Platelets.
    • Renal (Serum Creatinine/BUN) and Liver function panels.
Key monitoring
Monitoring Axis Clinical Indicators & Surveillance Targets Evaluation Protocol & Frequency
Thermal Profile & Fever Dynamics
  • Defervescence Trajectory: Fever resolution typically occurs within 24–48 hours of starting effective antimalarial therapy.
  • Recurrent/Persistent Fever: Spike in temperature after initial clearance may signal treatment failure, drug resistance, secondary bacterial infection, or drug reaction.
  • Measure core body temperature every 4 hours during the acute phase until stable for 24 hours.
  • In severe cases, actively cool with paracetamol/tepid sponging when temperature ≥ 38.5°C to prevent febrile seizures (especially in pediatrics).
Parasitaemia Clearance & Dynamics
  • Parasite Clearance Rate: Rapid reduction in asexual parasite density on thick blood film.
  • Therapeutic Response Marker: Parasitemia should decline by > 75% at 48 hours relative to baseline, with complete clearance by Day 3 (72 hours).
  • Perform quantitative blood film microscopy at 24, 48, and 72 hours post-treatment initiation in severe malaria or high initial parasitemia (> 2%).
  • If parasitemia remains > 25% of baseline at 48 hours or parasites persist at Day 3, evaluate for artemisinin partial resistance or non-adherence.
Hydration & Fluid Homeostasis
  • Euvolemia Maintenance: Balance the risk of hypovolemic shock/prerenal azotemia against fluid overload causing iatrogenic pulmonary edema.
  • Target Urine Output: > 1 mL/kg/hr in pediatric patients; > 0.5 mL/kg/hr in adult patients.
  • Maintain strict fluid intake/output charts for all inpatient cases.
  • Monitor blood pressure, jugular venous pressure (JVP), respiratory rate, and lung basilar auscultation every 2–4 hours during IV fluid administration.
Severe Disease Organ Surveillance
  • Glycemic Status: Screen for hypoglycemia (blood glucose < 3 mmol/L or < 54 mg/dL), exacerbated by IV quinine/artesunate or starvation.
  • Renal & Hematologic Function: Track worsening anemia (Hb/Hct), intravascular hemolysis (blackwater fever), acute kidney injury (creatinine), and metabolic acidosis (lactate).
  • Check blood glucose every 4 hours in unconscious or severely ill patients, and every 2 hours if receiving IV quinine.
  • Perform daily laboratory screening (CBC, electrolytes, BUN, creatinine, and arterial/venous blood gases) until acute clinical recovery.
Management principles
Clinical Scenario First-Line Antimalarial Regimens Therapeutic Considerations & Specific Instructions
Uncomplicated P. falciparum
  • Artemisinin-based Combination Therapies (ACTs) - 3-day oral course:
    • Artemether + Lumefantrine (AL)
    • Artesunate + Amodiaquine (AS+AQ)
    • Dihydroartemisinin + Piperaquine (DHA-PPQ)
    • Artesunate + Mefloquine (ASMQ)
    • Artesunate + Pyronaridine (ASPY)
  • Administer Artemether-Lumefantrine (AL) with fatty food or milk to ensure optimal drug absorption.
  • In low-transmission settings, add a single low dose of Primaquine (0.25 mg/kg) on Day 1 as a gametocytocidal agent to block transmission (no G6PD testing required for this single low dose).
  • Select ACT according to regional drug-resistance profiles and national guidelines.
Non-falciparum Species
  • Chloroquine-Sensitive Areas: Oral Chloroquine (total 25 mg base/kg over 3 days) OR an ACT.
  • Chloroquine-Resistant P. vivax: First-line treatment with an ACT (e.g., AL or DHA-PPQ).
  • Anti-relapse (Hypnozoitocidal) Therapy for P. vivax / P. ovale:
    • Primaquine (14-day daily course), OR
    • Tafenoquine (Single dose; age ≥ 16 years).
  • Mandatory G6PD Screening: Perform G6PD testing prior to initiating Primaquine or Tafenoquine to avoid severe intravascular hemolysis.
  • If G6PD deficient, provide weekly Primaquine (0.75 mg base/kg) for 8 weeks under close clinical supervision.
  • Contraindicated in pregnancy and infants < 6 months for Primaquine, and during pregnancy/lactation or age < 16 years for Tafenoquine.
Pregnancy Considerations
  • 1st Trimester (Uncomplicated): Oral Artemether-Lumefantrine (AL) is recommended by WHO as first-line therapy. (Quinine + Clindamycin for 7 days is an alternative option).
  • 2nd & 3rd Trimesters (Uncomplicated): Any standard first-line ACT (e.g., AL, DHA-PPQ, or AS+AQ).
  • Avoid
Follow-up / outcomes
Follow-up Domain Clinical Targets & Complication Risks Monitoring Timelines & Actions
Clinical Response & Therapeutic Outcome
  • Expected Progress: Resolution of fever within 24–48 hours and complete symptom recovery by Day 3–7.
  • Treatment Failure Indicators: Persistence or recrudescence of fever, clinical deterioration, or persistent parasitemia at Day 3 post-treatment.
  • Schedule routine clinical review at Day 3, Day 7, Day 14, and Day 28 (or Day 42 for long half-life drugs like Piperaquine).
  • Perform repeat thick/thin blood microscopy at Day 3 and at any unscheduled visit if symptoms recur.
Glycemic & Metabolic Surveillance
  • Severe Hypoglycemia Risk: Blood glucose < 3.0 mmol/L (< 54 mg/dL) secondary to hyperinsulinemia (quinine-induced) or hepatic glycogen depletion.
  • Acidosis & Electrolyte Imbalance: High blood lactate levels or metabolic acidosis driven by tissue hypoxia and microvascular obstruction.
  • Check blood glucose every 2–4 hours during acute severe illness or IV treatment (every 2 hours during IV quinine therapy).
  • Treat acute hypoglycemia immediately with IV 10% or 50% Dextrose bolus, followed by continuous maintenance glucose infusion.
Renal Monitoring & Hemodynamic Status
  • Acute Kidney Injury (AKI): Oliguria/anuria, elevated serum creatinine, and urea caused by acute tubular necrosis or severe intravascular hemolysis (Blackwater fever).
  • Fluid Overload: Prone to iatrogenic pulmonary edema due to aggressive fluid resuscitation in capillary-leak states.
  • Monitor strict fluid balance and urine output daily (target > 0.5 mL/kg/hr for adults).
  • Track serum creatinine and BUN daily in severe disease until renal function stabilizes; initiate renal replacement therapy (RRT/hemodialysis) early if severe AKI or hyperkalemia occurs.
Late Complications & Relapse Prevention
  • Post-Artesunate Delayed Hemolysis (PADH): Unexplained drop in hemoglobin occurring 1–4 weeks following IV artesunate therapy for severe malaria.
  • Relapse Surveillance: Relapse from dormant liver hypnozoites in P. vivax or P. ovale infection.
  • Obtain weekly Hemoglobin/Hematocrit levels for 4 weeks following IV Artesunate administration.
  • Educate patients to re-attend immediately if dark/tea-colored urine, jaundice, pallor, or recurring fever occurs.
Patient education
Education Domain Core Clinical Explanation & Counseling Messaging Verification via Teach-Back Prompts
Diagnosis & Treatment Purpose
  • Condition Explanation: Clearly explain that malaria is a blood infection transmitted by mosquito bites and confirmed by laboratory testing.
  • Therapeutic Goal: Explain that antimalarial medication clears parasites from the blood, prevents organ complications, and stops the infection from returning.
  • "To make sure I explained everything clearly, how would you describe what malaria is and why taking this full course of medication is necessary?"
Adherence & Administration
  • Full Course Completion: Emphasize completing the entire 3-day (or 14-day anti-relapse) regimen even if feeling completely well after 24 hours.
  • Dosing Requirements: Advise taking Artemether-Lumefantrine (AL) with fatty food or milk to ensure the drug is absorbed properly into the body.
  • Vomiting Management: Instructions to take a full repeat dose if vomiting occurs within 30 minutes of swallowing the tablet.
  • "Can you walk me through how and when you will take your pills over the next three days, especially regarding meals?"
  • "What will you do if you vomit shortly after taking your dose?"
Vector Control & Vector Prevention
  • Bed Net Usage: Sleep under Insecticide-Treated Nets (ITNs) every night.
  • Bite Prevention: Use DEET/Picaridin insect repellents, wear long sleeves/pants during peak mosquito biting hours (dusk to dawn), and use window screens.
  • Environmental Control: Eliminate standing water around the home to disrupt mosquito breeding sites.
  • "What are two steps you plan to take at home to protect yourself and your family from mosquito bites?"
Monitoring & Urgent Red Flags
  • Routine Follow-up: Review the necessity of returning for follow-up blood tests as scheduled.
  • Urgent Red Flags: Re-attend the hospital immediately if experiencing:
    • Persistent vomiting or inability to keep liquids down.
    • Extreme weakness, confusion, or inability to sit/walk.
    • Dark/tea-colored urine or yellowing of eyes (jaundice).
    • Shortness of breath or rapid, labored breathing.
    • Recurrent high fever despite taking all medication.
  • "What are three warning signs that would tell you to stop waiting and come straight back to the clinic or emergency room?"

Individualization
Patient Factor Clinical Impact & Vulnerabilities Tailoring Strategy & Regimen Adjustments
Age Extremes (Pediatrics & Elderly)
  • Infants & Young Children: High risk of rapid progression to severe disease, high parasitemia, severe anemia, and hypoglycemic convulsions.
  • Elderly: Increased risk of acute renal impairment and cardiac toxicity/arrhythmia.
  • Use dispersible pediatric ACT formulations for infants to ensure accurate milligram-per-kilogram dosing.
  • Adjust dose according to precise weight rather than age bands wherever feasible.
Pregnancy & Lactation
  • Increased risk of severe malaria, maternal anemia, spontaneous abortion, low birth weight, and severe hypoglycemia (especially on quinine).
  • Use Artemether-Lumefantrine (AL) as first-line across all trimesters for uncomplicated malaria per WHO guidelines.
  • Contraindicate Primaquine and Tafenoquine during pregnancy and breastfeeding unless infant G6PD status is confirmed normal.

Patient Checklist

  • Know the name and purpose of each medicine.
  • Know how and when to take or use treatment.
  • Ask what adverse effects require medical attention.
  • Keep appointments and monitoring tests.
  • Tell the healthcare team about OTC medicines, supplements and allergies.
  • Do not stop long-term treatment abruptly without professional advice unless an emergency clinician instructs otherwise.
